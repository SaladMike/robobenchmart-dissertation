\section{Conclusions}

This dissertation set out to build a retail mobile-manipulation benchmark and to find out what current generalist policies do inside it. It completed the first half. The second half ran into a constraint that has to be stated before anything else, because it governs how every number in the results chapter should be read: all adaptation for this study was carried out on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090.

That card permitted a batch size of eight, step budgets between thirty and a hundred thousand, and low-rank adaptation rather than full fine-tuning for three of the four models. Each run stopped when its preset counter expired, no schedule was searched, and none was assessed with a validation-based convergence criterion. The main table therefore describes four fixed-step study pipelines whose training sufficiency was not established. No policy exceeds 4\% on any task under Pose, and the triplet-level Seeds/Pose/Layout table spans zero to nine successes in a hundred trials. Task-level cells pool 200 or 300 trials, depending on scenario and task, and all calculations use the unrounded counts rather than displayed integer percentages. Marginal intervals for individual cells would not constitute tests of architecture, task or condition differences; adaptation recipes and task definitions remain unmatched. The study can report what these four pipelines achieved after a few hours of single-card adaptation. It cannot report how well the underlying architectures can learn retail manipulation, and it does not claim to.

What follows from that is a narrower set of conclusions than the study was designed to produce, and they are worth stating in the order of how well they are supported.

RQ1 is answered for procedural variation, collision constraints and geometric passage provision. Store plans generate with structured variation and instantiate in ManiSkill3, while mesh simplification moves the practical ceiling from four shelving units to a hundred and eighteen. Visual plausibility was inspected rather than measured, and store-scale navigation was not exercised by the reported episodes. The engineering half of RQ2 is answered more directly: the demonstration collector turned task specifications into 2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions in about ten hours without a teleoperation rig, and one observation--action schema fed four architectures with almost nothing in common internally.

The second half of RQ2---whether that corpus suffices to adapt generalist policies---is answered only in the weakest sense. In the triplet-level Seeds/Pose/Layout table---four models by three modes by twelve task--item--fixture combinations---111 of 144 cells contain at least one success; Pairing and composite-task cells are not included. The runs did not produce dependable behaviour in any model. The study does not separate the effects of the corpus, compute, optimisation configuration and task difficulty.

RQ3 is not answered. The Pose--Layout point differences can be calculated from the pooled counts: ten of the twenty model--task pairs are exactly unchanged, nine move by no more than two thirds of a percentage point, and Octo's door-closing value rises by 2.5 points, from 8/200 to 13/200. Pose success is only 0--4\%, however, so the observable downward range is sharply floor-limited, and the retained aggregates do not support design-aware intervals for the condition contrasts. The door comparison is narrower still because its Layout condition does not change scene geometry. The study therefore cannot distinguish a small degradation from no change or improvement. Whether current generalist policies transfer across retail-specific shift remains open in both directions, and Section~\ref{sec:ablations} specifies the controlled experiment that would close it.

Two findings survive the collapse of the success column because they never depended on it. The aggregate failure distribution shows descriptive differences across the four constrained runs: SmolVLA stops before contact, with 40\% of its failures at pre-grasp, while the $\pi$ models more often reach the object and fail at grasp. And two properties of the evaluation setup bear directly on that grasp bottleneck regardless of training budget---a policy is queried once and then executes fifty actions blind, and the torso is pinned at its lowest position for every episode of every task, so vertical reach is a constant of the benchmark rather than a degree of freedom.

Independent verification identified several differences between the intended protocol and its execution, and each retained discrepancy was reproduced before interpretation. The products called unseen were on the shelves throughout training. The scenes called unseen were, for four of the seven atomic tasks, the training scenes. The robot began every episode 1.4~metres from the correct fixture and facing it, so store-scale navigation was never exercised. Basket picking used a forty-centimetre acceptance box and checked collateral displacement only on the target fixture; several stores that were meant to differ were also generated from identical seeds. Against that, two aspects were harsher than described: the policy was blind for fifty simulator steps at a stretch and the torso never moved.

Nearly everything on the longer list made the test more forgiving, and the scores were near zero anyway. That rules out an interpretation in which the intended distribution shifts alone caused the low rates. It does not identify what did: fixed-step training without convergence checks remains confounded with the corpus, optimisation configuration and task difficulty. The result is not evidence that the tasks are impossible, and it should not be quoted as such.

The contribution, then, is an implemented benchmark whose generation, simulation, collection and interface components were exercised, together with a policy study whose training sufficiency was not established and a documented account of both. It is not a new architecture or a new metric. The platform supports a controlled retail-manipulation study, but the transfer claim still requires a convergence-checked adaptation run with the corrected protocol.

\section{Limitations}

These are limits of design rather than defects of execution, which makes them no less binding. The differences between the documented protocol and the experiment as executed are separate and are reported in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

Everything here is simulated on one principal embodiment. Real products bring deformable labels, transparent packaging, uncertain friction, sensor noise, calibration error and damage modes that no amount of rendering fidelity supplies. Human workers and moving equipment are absent entirely. The task set covers picking and articulation but stops there: no scanning, no deformable bags, no inventory verification, no requirement to behave safely around a person.

The statistics are thinner than the number of episodes suggests, and at the observed rates they are thinner still. A hundred trials per triplet could have supported bootstrap intervals over seeds, paired comparisons on shared episode configurations and a mixed-effects treatment of item and layout variance if the episode-level pairing and grouping had been retained. They were not. The task-level table therefore gives rounded point estimates pooled over 200 or 300 trials, and marginal intervals for individual rates would not repair the missing design-aware contrasts or justify a capability ranking across unmatched adaptation recipes and heterogeneous tasks. The failure taxonomy has a related weakness: one annotator, no second pass, no agreement statistic and an earliest-primary rule that deliberately hides downstream damage.

Unverified training sufficiency is the binding limitation of the policy study. All four runs used one RTX 4090, batch size 8 and preset step budgets; three used low-rank adaptation, and none continued to a validation-based convergence criterion. The cross-model table therefore compares four fixed-step pipelines rather than four architectures trained to a common standard. A convergence-checked comparison is needed before low performance can be attributed to compute, data, optimisation or task difficulty.

Composite tasks are decomposed by an oracle, which means their zeros say something about sequential execution and nothing about autonomous planning. The environments expose per-subtask progress, but those episode-level outputs were not retained in the archived results bundle. Partial progress cannot therefore be reconstructed for this dissertation without rerunning evaluation, and the reported evidence is limited to full-sequence completion.

\section{Future work}

Before anything is extended, one experiment should be run, and it is not a new one. Adapt a single policy with validation-based checkpoint selection until its in-domain success no longer improves, then re-evaluate it on the scenes and conditions already built. The resulting learning curve and held-out success rate would show whether the preset-step run stopped before measurable performance emerged. If performance remained near zero after that check, data, optimisation and task difficulty would still need controlled ablations before any one of them could be named as the limiting factor. Everything else in this section depends on establishing that training baseline first.

A corrected rerun should be folded into the same pass. It would enable the torso and head commands already present in the nominal thirteen-dimensional interface, generate genuinely disjoint evaluation scenes for the four door tasks, replace the duplicated configuration seeds, extend basket collateral checking to the whole scene and sweep the execution horizon rather than fixing it at fifty. Enabling those joints changes the effective control distribution even though it does not change the nominal action dimension. Because every reported demonstration and evaluation fixed torso lift and head pan at zero, the corrected comparison requires demonstrations and checkpoints trained under the enabled-control regime.

The most immediate extension after that is an automatic diagnostic evaluator. An evaluator using ground-truth simulator state could calculate target-selection accuracy, navigation reachability, pre-grasp pose error, grasp retention time, non-target displacement and conditional subtask success. Episode-level outputs would support confidence intervals and make failure analysis less dependent on manual labels.

Data collection should add recovery. Perturbing demonstrations at approach and grasp stages, then planning or teleoperating corrections, would teach the policy what to do after small deviations. A mixed corpus could retain efficient planned successes while adding human demonstrations where contact is difficult. Retail-specific pretraining is another promising hypothesis, but it should be tested through controlled data-scale and domain-mixture ablations rather than assumed from model ranking.

Scene generation can also move closer to measured reality. Store-plan statistics, calibrated camera noise, friction variation, compliant packaging and dynamic obstacles would improve external validity. Dynamic level of detail based on camera visibility could replace the current near-versus-background simplification rule. Finally, physical validation should begin with matched atomic tasks and identical success predicates, then quantify the sim-to-real gap before attempting a full autonomous order.

\section{A staged route to physical validation}

Physical validation should proceed in stages rather than attempt a complete dark-store deployment at once. The first stage would reconstruct one simulated shelf cell using matched product dimensions, a calibrated Fetch sensor arrangement and the same atomic success predicates. A small set of planned demonstrations could verify controller mapping and reveal the magnitude of mismatches in perception, friction and calibration. Results should be paired by item and initial pose so that the simulation-to-real gap is measured, not described impressionistically.

The next stage would introduce controlled variation one factor at a time: packaging gloss, shelf height, object mass, friction and camera exposure. Failures could then be compared with the simulator taxonomy. If physical episodes fail before grasp while their simulated counterparts reach contact, perception or calibration deserves priority. If both reach the item but only physical grasps slip, contact modelling and compliance are the more likely gap. This paired diagnostic use is more informative than reporting a single real-world success percentage.

Only after atomic transfer is understood should the system move into a full aisle and composite orders. Safety constraints would need explicit treatment: limited base speed, collision monitoring, emergency stop, exclusion zones and conservative recovery when a product or fixture state becomes uncertain. Human supervision requirements should be reported with throughput, because an apparently autonomous system that frequently requests intervention shifts rather than removes labour.

This staged route keeps the benchmark useful even if absolute simulated scores do not predict physical scores. Its controlled scenes provide hypotheses, repeatable starting states and failure vocabulary. Physical trials then test which parts transfer and which require a richer model. The outcome would be a calibrated benchmark rather than a claim that visual realism alone closes the sim-to-real gap.

Physical validation must also include the people affected by the system. Retail automation can reduce repetitive lifting and make fulfilment more consistent, but it can also relocate work into supervision, exception handling and equipment maintenance. A field study should therefore record interventions, near misses and recovery burden alongside robot throughput. Worker feedback can reveal hazards that are invisible in simulation, such as blocked escape routes, awkward manual resets or uncertainty about when the robot will move. These observations belong in system evaluation rather than a separate afterthought.

Data governance is relevant even when the benchmark itself is synthetic. A deployed robot may capture workers, customer information or commercially sensitive shelf state. Camera retention, access control and model logging should be specified before collection begins. Synthetic pretraining reduces some privacy exposure, but physical evaluation reintroduces it. The responsible objective is not maximum autonomy at any cost; it is a system whose benefits, failure modes and human responsibilities can be measured and challenged.

The benchmark's hardest result is also its most useful one: a policy can look general on broad pretraining data and remain fragile at a shelf. By making that fragility reproducible and diagnosable, the proposed benchmark provides a concrete starting point for work that aims to close it.

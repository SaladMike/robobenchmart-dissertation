\section{Conclusions}

This dissertation set out to build a retail mobile-manipulation benchmark and to find out what current generalist policies do inside it. It completed the first half. The second half ran into a constraint that has to be stated before anything else, because it governs how every number in the results chapter should be read: all adaptation for this study was carried out on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090.

That card permitted a batch size of eight, step budgets between thirty and a hundred thousand, and low-rank adaptation rather than full fine-tuning for three of the four models. No run was carried to the point where its loss stopped falling, and no schedule was searched. The consequence is visible in every cell of the main table. No policy exceeds 4\% on any task under the mildest evaluation condition; the entire per-item table spans zero to nine successes in a hundred trials; and the confidence intervals of every pair of cells in the task-level main table overlap. The study can therefore report what these four architectures do after a few hours of single-card adaptation. It cannot report how well they learn retail manipulation, and it does not claim to.

What follows from that is a narrower set of conclusions than the study was designed to produce, and they are worth stating in the order of how well they are supported.

The instrument works, and RQ1 and the first half of RQ2 are answered yes on their own terms. Store plans generate with structured variation, stay collision-free and navigable, and instantiate in ManiSkill3. Mesh simplification moves the practical ceiling from four shelving units to a hundred and eighteen, which is the difference between a corner of a shop and a shop. The demonstration collector turned task specifications into 2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions in about ten hours without a teleoperation rig, and one observation--action schema fed four architectures with almost nothing in common internally.

The second half of RQ2---whether that corpus suffices to adapt generalist policies---is answered only in the weakest sense. It elicited retail behaviour that is not zero: 111 of the 144 per-item cells record at least one success. It did not produce dependable behaviour in any model. Whether the binding constraint was the corpus or the compute, this study cannot separate, and the compute is the more likely of the two.

RQ3 is not answered. Measuring how policies behave as evaluation moves from familiar conditions to unseen scenes requires performance in the familiar condition to move away from, and there is none. Across twenty model--task pairs the change from Pose to Layout is zero percentage points in fifteen and one point in four. The shift conditions are built, the scenes generate, the protocol runs; the policies were too weakly adapted for the measurement to discriminate. Whether current generalist policies transfer across retail-specific shift remains open in both directions, and Section~\ref{sec:ablations} specifies the single experiment that would close it.

Two findings survive the collapse of the success column because they never depended on it. The aggregate failure distribution shows descriptive differences across the four constrained runs: SmolVLA stops before contact, with 40\% of its failures at pre-grasp, while the $\pi$ models more often reach the object and fail at grasp. And two properties of the evaluation setup bear directly on that grasp bottleneck regardless of training budget---a policy is queried once and then executes fifty actions blind, and the torso is pinned at its lowest position for every episode of every task, so vertical reach is a constant of the benchmark rather than a degree of freedom.

One further result was not planned. During the production of the initial results, the experimental implementation was examined independently by a fellow student, and I reproduced each retained discrepancy myself before revising the interpretation. The resulting list is lopsided. The products called unseen were on the shelves throughout training. The scenes called unseen were, for four of the seven atomic tasks, the training scenes. The robot began every episode 1.4~metres from the correct fixture and facing it, so store-scale navigation was never in the experiment at all. The acceptance box is forty centimetres across; the collateral-damage check is narrower than the equation that describes it; and several stores that were meant to differ were generated from identical seeds. Against that, two aspects were harsher than described: the policy was blind for fifty simulator steps at a stretch and the torso never moved.

Nearly everything on the longer list made the test more forgiving, and the scores were near zero anyway. That combination points in one direction. A test made easier than advertised, returning almost nothing from policies whose training was cut short by a step counter, is a test whose limiting factor sits on the training side. It is not evidence that the tasks are impossible, and it should not be quoted as such.

The contribution, then, is a benchmark that works, a policy study that the available compute could not carry, and a documented account of both. It is not a new architecture or a new metric. A reader who wants one sentence from this work should take this one: the instrument is ready, and running the experiment it was built for needs more compute than this dissertation had.

\section{Limitations}

These are limits of design rather than defects of execution, which makes them no less binding. The differences between the documented protocol and the experiment as executed are separate and are reported in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

Everything here is simulated on one principal embodiment. Real products bring deformable labels, transparent packaging, uncertain friction, sensor noise, calibration error and damage modes that no amount of rendering fidelity supplies. Human workers and moving equipment are absent entirely. The task set covers picking and articulation but stops there: no scanning, no deformable bags, no inventory verification, no requirement to behave safely around a person.

The statistics are thinner than the number of episodes suggests, and at the observed rates they are thinner still. A hundred trials per triplet would support bootstrap intervals over seeds, paired comparisons between models on shared episode configurations and a mixed-effects treatment of item and layout variance. None of that is reported. Point estimates are reported, and the pairwise Wilson intervals across the task-level main table overlap, so no difference between any two of its cells should be read as a difference in capability. The failure taxonomy has a related weakness: one annotator, no second pass, no agreement statistic and an earliest-primary rule that deliberately hides downstream damage.

Compute is the binding limitation of this study and is stated first among the limitations for that reason. A single RTX 4090 forced a batch size of eight, short step budgets and low-rank adaptation on three of the four models, and every run stopped on a step counter rather than on any evidence that it had finished learning. The cross-model table therefore compares four pipelines whose training was cut short by the same constraint; it does not compare four architectures, and it cannot be used the other way. Nothing in the design of the benchmark caused this, and nothing in the benchmark needs to change to remove it.

Composite tasks are decomposed by an oracle, which means their zeros say something about sequential execution and nothing about autonomous planning. Worse, the zeros were reported as zeros when the environments had already computed how far each episode got. That was an error in this dissertation, not a property of the benchmark, and it is the one item on this list that could be fixed this week.

\section{Future work}

Before anything is extended, one experiment should be run, and it is not a new one. Adapt a single policy until it stops improving, then re-evaluate it on the scenes and conditions already built. SmolVLA at 450M parameters is the natural candidate for a single card: train it until in-domain success stops improving rather than until a step counter expires, then read the existing Seeds, Pose, Layout and Pairing columns. If its in-domain rate reaches even twenty percent, every comparison the results chapter had to withdraw becomes measurable again with no change to the benchmark. If it does not, the attribution moves from the budget to the tasks, and that would be a finding in its own right. Everything else in this section assumes one or the other of those outcomes.

A corrected rerun should be folded into the same pass. It would restore the torso to the action space, unpin the head, generate genuinely disjoint evaluation scenes for the four door tasks, replace the duplicated configuration seeds, widen the collateral check to the whole scene and sweep the execution horizon rather than fixing it at fifty. Most of those are configuration edits and cost minutes. Restoring the torso is not: it changes the action space and invalidates every checkpoint at once. Since the checkpoints reported here would have to be retrained for the budget experiment regardless, the two are cheaper together than either is alone.

The most immediate extension after that is an automatic diagnostic evaluator. An evaluator using ground-truth simulator state could calculate target-selection accuracy, navigation reachability, pre-grasp pose error, grasp retention time, non-target displacement and conditional subtask success. Episode-level outputs would support confidence intervals and make failure analysis less dependent on manual labels.

Data collection should add recovery. Perturbing demonstrations at approach and grasp stages, then planning or teleoperating corrections, would teach the policy what to do after small deviations. A mixed corpus could retain efficient planned successes while adding human demonstrations where contact is difficult. Retail-specific pretraining is another promising hypothesis, but it should be tested through controlled data-scale and domain-mixture ablations rather than assumed from model ranking.

Scene generation can also move closer to measured reality. Store-plan statistics, calibrated camera noise, friction variation, compliant packaging and dynamic obstacles would improve external validity. Dynamic level of detail based on camera visibility could replace the current near-versus-background simplification rule. Finally, physical validation should begin with matched atomic tasks and identical success predicates, then quantify the sim-to-real gap before attempting a full autonomous order.

\section{A staged route to physical validation}

Physical validation should proceed in stages rather than attempt a complete dark-store deployment at once. The first stage would reconstruct one simulated shelf cell using matched product dimensions, a calibrated Fetch sensor arrangement and the same atomic success predicates. A small set of planned demonstrations could verify controller mapping and reveal the magnitude of mismatches in perception, friction and calibration. Results should be paired by item and initial pose so that the simulation-to-real gap is measured, not described impressionistically.

The next stage would introduce controlled variation one factor at a time: packaging gloss, shelf height, object mass, friction and camera exposure. Failures could then be compared with the simulator taxonomy. If physical episodes fail before grasp while their simulated counterparts reach contact, perception or calibration deserves priority. If both reach the item but only physical grasps slip, contact modelling and compliance are the more likely gap. This paired diagnostic use is more informative than reporting a single real-world success percentage.

Only after atomic transfer is understood should the system move into a full aisle and composite orders. Safety constraints would need explicit treatment: limited base speed, collision monitoring, emergency stop, exclusion zones and conservative recovery when a product or fixture state becomes uncertain. Human supervision requirements should be reported with throughput, because an apparently autonomous system that frequently requests intervention shifts rather than removes labour.

This staged route keeps the benchmark useful even if absolute simulated scores do not predict physical scores. Its controlled scenes provide hypotheses, repeatable starting states and failure vocabulary. Physical trials then test which parts transfer and which require a richer model. The outcome would be a calibrated benchmark rather than a claim that visual realism alone closes the sim-to-real gap.

Physical validation must also include the people affected by the system. Retail automation can reduce repetitive lifting and make fulfilment more consistent, but it can also relocate work into supervision, exception handling and equipment maintenance. A field study should therefore record interventions, near misses and recovery burden alongside robot throughput. Worker feedback can reveal hazards that are invisible in simulation, such as blocked escape routes, awkward manual resets or uncertainty about when the robot will move. These observations belong in system evaluation rather than a separate afterthought.

Data governance is relevant even when the benchmark itself is synthetic. A deployed robot may capture workers, customer information or commercially sensitive shelf state. Camera retention, access control and model logging should be specified before collection begins. Synthetic pretraining reduces some privacy exposure, but physical evaluation reintroduces it. The responsible objective is not maximum autonomy at any cost; it is a system whose benefits, failure modes and human responsibilities can be measured and challenged.

The benchmark's hardest result is also its most useful one: a policy can look general on broad pretraining data and remain fragile at a shelf. By making that fragility reproducible and diagnosable, the proposed benchmark provides a concrete starting point for work that aims to close it.

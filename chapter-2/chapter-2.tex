\section{What a benchmark is for}

It is easy to think of a robot benchmark as a set of tasks with a leaderboard attached. I think that undersells it. A benchmark is a promise about comparison: it says what varies between episodes, what is held fixed, how success is decided, and therefore which claims a difference in scores can support. In robot learning the promise is fragile, because the cameras, the embodiment, the controller and even the way a scene is reset can each make a task look harder or easier than it is.

Physical trials remain the most convincing kind of evidence, and the most awkward to repeat. Objects wear. Cameras get nudged. One person resets a scene a little differently from the next, and a failed grasp can leave the table in a state that changes the following episode. Simulation gives up some physical fidelity in exchange for exact resets, access to privileged state and the ability to run counterfactuals, so that one policy can meet a new floor texture, a different store plan or a different product split while everything else stays put. That bargain only pays off if the simulator keeps the geometric and contact constraints that make the target domain hard in the first place.

Existing suites have struck the bargain in different places. Meta-World and robosuite offer many tabletop tasks and interchangeable controllers, and robomimic uses that kind of setting to study learning from offline human demonstrations in a systematic way~\cite{yu2020metaworld,zhu2020robosuite,mandlekar2021whatmatters}. ManiSkill2 widens the range of robots, object models and manipulation families~\cite{gu2023maniskill2}. Move up to room scale and iGibson 2.0 and Habitat 2.0 put mobile manipulation inside interactive homes with articulated furniture~\cite{li2022igibson2,szot2021habitat2}. HomeRobot goes further and asks a single agent to navigate, grasp a novel object and place it, all from an open-vocabulary instruction~\cite{yenamandra2023homerobot}, while ProcTHOR attacks a different bottleneck altogether by generating interactive training houses in bulk~\cite{deitke2022procthor}. Between them they show that task diversity, physical interaction and controlled generalisation can live in one system. Almost all of it happens on tabletops or in homes.

There is also the question of what simulated success means for a real robot. RoboTHOR addresses it directly, pairing simulated rooms with physical replicas so that the drop from one to the other can be measured under matched conditions~\cite{deitke2020robothor}; its first benchmark, though, is visual navigation, not contact-rich manipulation. The general point stands. A repeatable simulated result is evidence about the simulated task. It is not, by itself, evidence that a robot could do the job.

Why should the home-and-tabletop bias matter for retail? Because a supermarket shelf is a different kind of clutter. The same product appears many times over. Neighbouring packages can differ by a word on the label, clearances are tight, and a careless arm can knock several items out of place at once. The aisles are narrow and long. To fetch ``the Fanta bottle'' a policy has to pick it out from other bright, branded bottles and then arrive at a pose from which the gripper can actually reach it. Doing well in a kitchen does not guarantee any of that. A retail benchmark earns its place when the domain changes the geometry, the appearance and the ways things go wrong, and not merely the wallpaper.

\section{Retail datasets and the missing interaction layer}

Retail vision already has large datasets. They just answer different questions. RPC captures checkout scenes with many products in view; Products-10K is about telling apart 10,000 fine-grained stock-keeping units~\cite{wei2019rpc,bai2020products10k}. The Grocery Store Dataset takes its images inside real shops and organises its labels hierarchically, and the Freiburg Groceries Dataset works at a coarser category level~\cite{klasson2019grocery,jund2016freiburg}. All of them make perception hard in the right ways, with near-identical packaging, clutter and shifts between capture conditions. None of them can say whether the product the model picked out is within reach, whether the gripper would clip the box beside it, or whether the item would stay upright once put down. Their labels end where the action begins.

Three-dimensional assets get closer. YCB and ShapeNet have served manipulation and shape research for years~\cite{calli2015ycb,chang2015shapenet}, and Google Scanned Objects added more than a thousand simulation-ready scans of household items~\cite{francis2022gso}. Collections aimed at retail fit the domain better: IPA-3D1K reconstructs real retail products, and 3D-FRONT shows how textured assets and professionally designed layouts can be bundled into complete synthetic interiors~\cite{lindermayr2023ipa,fu2021threeDfront}. Still, a pile of meshes is not a shop. The assets need consistent units, orientations, collision shapes and materials. Then someone has to put them on shelves, in rows, the way stock is actually faced, and keep the polygon count low enough that the scene still renders at interactive rates. Licensing and access add their own friction.

Shelf picking has recently started to attract generative, simulation-first approaches. FetchBot, for instance, concentrates on densely packed shelves, on retrieving items without disturbing their neighbours, and on transferring the result zero-shot to a physical arm~\cite{liu2025fetchbot}. That is a hard and useful problem. It is also a single shelf: there is no store to cross, no change of layout and no fridge door to open.

The nearest neighbours of RoboBenchMart sit on either side of it. FetchBench procedurally builds constrained fetching scenes, with shelves, drawers, cabinets and baskets, and scores the whole approach--grasp--retrieve chain for both modular and learned baselines~\cite{pmlr-v270-han25a}; it is about objects and geometry, and retail is incidental. Sari Sandbox comes from the opposite direction, with three virtual grocery stores, more than 250 interactive products, an embodied agent driven through an API and the SariBench set of human shopping demonstrations~\cite{gajo2025sari}. MarketGen generates whole supermarkets from text or reference images, reports over 1,100 goods and evaluates both checkout unloading and in-aisle mobile manipulation, including a sim-to-real study~\cite{hu2025marketgen}. RoboBenchMart, the benchmark I use, combines procedurally generated dark-store layouts, stocked fixtures and motion-planned demonstrations in ManiSkill3, and reports fine-tuned VLA baselines under four evaluation conditions~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. \ref{tab:retail-benchmark-comparison} lays them side by side. I have not tried to rank them, and the last row is there to make clear how much smaller the present study's role is.

Read together, these resources leave a gap with layers to it. Two-dimensional images of products are plentiful. Three-dimensional retail assets are scarcer, and systems that turn those assets into repeatable manipulation episodes are scarcer still. What is mostly missing is the interaction layer: a task interface, a controller, a success predicate and a path to training data, so that the question can move from ``which product is visible?'' to ``can the robot do what it was asked without breaking anything?'' RoboBenchMart tries to supply that layer. What happens when someone outside the original team actually runs it is what this dissertation sets out to record.

\section{Procedural environments and structured variation}

A hand-built scene, however detailed, can be memorised. Procedural generation is the standard answer, but random placement on its own produces nonsense: fixtures overlap, aisles close up, and the result looks like a warehouse after an earthquake rather than a shop. The systems that work all impose structure of some kind. ProcTHOR scales interactive houses through procedural rules; Infinigen Indoors pairs a constraint language with generated assets; Holodeck turns a text description into spatial constraints over retrieved objects~\cite{deitke2022procthor,raistrick2024infinigenindoors,yang2024holodeck}. Their machinery differs a great deal. Their lesson is the same. More scenes help only if each one is still plausible.

RoboBenchMart's fixture placement borrows from tensor-field methods for procedural street modelling~\cite{chen2008interactive}. There, a field assigns a preferred orientation to every point of a map and roads are traced along it, which yields street networks that bend coherently instead of wandering. Store shelving poses a similar problem in miniature, since shelves run in rows, follow walls and leave passages between them. A second-order tensor suits the job because it encodes an axis rather than a heading. A shelf at $0^{\circ}$ and a shelf at $180^{\circ}$ are the same shelf, and the tensor does not pretend otherwise. Chapter~\ref{chap:system} looks at how the upstream code actually uses the field, which turns out to be more discrete than the formulation suggests, and at the alternatives it was chosen over.

Filling the shelves brings a separate set of constraints. Products have to rest on support surfaces, usually in tidy groups; the \texttt{scene\_synthesizer} library offers operations of exactly this kind~\cite{eppner2025scene}. Then there is cost. High-resolution product meshes multiply fast, and a few hundred of them per store can swallow memory and rendering time. Remeshing, for example with QuadriFlow~\cite{huang2018quadriflow}, or cruder primitive approximations are the usual cures, and RoboBenchMart reports keeping full meshes only on the shelf nearest the robot~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. Nobody would call this a learning contribution. It nonetheless decides which experiments are affordable.

\section{Generating robot demonstrations}

Fine-tuning a policy requires trajectories, and someone or something has to produce them. Teleoperation gives natural-looking behaviour and copes with awkward contact, but the effort grows with every new task and scene, and on hardware each attempt also has to be reset by hand. In simulation the planner can see exact object poses and collision models, so collection can be automated. What is gained in scale is partly lost in how human the motion looks.

Sampling-based motion planning delivers collision-free paths without any learned component~\cite{LaValle_2006}. RRT-Connect, the workhorse for single queries, grows one tree from the start configuration and another from the goal until they meet~\cite{kuffner2000rrt}, and OMPL wraps it and many relatives behind a common interface~\cite{sucan2012ompl}. On GPUs, cuRobo takes a different path, combining parallel geometric search, inverse kinematics and trajectory optimisation~\cite{sundaralingam2023curobo}. Asymptotically optimal planners give stronger guarantees and cost more to run~\cite{KaramanSamplingBased}. For collecting demonstrations, though, optimality is beside the point. What counts is a planner that succeeds often, varies its solutions and is judged by a clear acceptance test.

RoboBenchMart's collector is built on that pragmatic footing. Hand-written task logic sets waypoints for approach, grasp, transport and placement. Between waypoints the planner tries a screw motion~\cite{murray2017mathematical} and falls back to RRT-Connect if it fails, and only episodes that end in success are kept. That scales far better than teleoperation and, because generation is seeded, can in principle be repeated exactly. Its limits are just as easy to see. The waypoints carry a person's understanding of the task into the data. Keeping only successes skews the distribution: the policy sees how to finish a task, never how to recover from a slip. MimicGen takes another route, multiplying a handful of human demonstrations by transforming them across scenes and object poses~\cite{mandlekar2023mimicgen}. Reinforcement learning can discover behaviour from reward~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,haarnoja2017soft}, but for long mobile-manipulation tasks the reward design, exploration and compute remain heavy. None of these routes rules out the others; they can be, and are, combined.

There is a well-known catch in learning from demonstrations that contain only successes. A policy cloned from expert trajectories sees only the states the expert visited. At run time its own small mistakes carry it into states the expert never visited, where it has nothing to imitate, and the errors compound. Ross et al.\ analysed this distribution shift and proposed DAgger, in which the expert is queried on the states the learner actually reaches~\cite{ross2011dagger}. A planner-generated corpus that keeps only successful episodes is exposed to this problem in a pure form. It contains no near-misses, no corrections and no second attempts. What follows from that for the checkpoints evaluated here is an interpretation, not a measurement, but it is a natural one. A policy deployed in an environment that differs slightly from the one it was trained in, say in the arm's starting posture, may be off the demonstration distribution from the first step.

\section{Generalist vision-language-action policies}

No single laboratory can collect data for every robot and every task, and large pooled datasets such as Open X-Embodiment and DROID are the response~\cite{open_x_embodiment_rt_x_2023,khazatsky2024droid}. Trained on them, generalist policies condition their actions on camera images, a language instruction and the robot's own state, and share what they learn across embodiments.

The designs vary widely. Octo is a transformer policy built to be adapted to new robots and tasks~\cite{octo_2023}. OpenVLA puts a vision-language backbone in front of autoregressive action tokens~\cite{kim24openvla}, while Dita scales up a diffusion transformer instead~\cite{hou2025dita}. The $\pi_0$ family joins a vision-language model to a flow-matching action expert~\cite{black2024pi0visionlanguageactionflowmodel}, and $\pi_{0.5}$ pushes that family towards open-world generalisation~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld}. SmolVLA heads the other way, towards a model small enough to train and run cheaply~\cite{shukor2025smolvlavisionlanguageactionmodelaffordable}. Scale, pretraining data, action representation and fine-tuning recipe all differ between them. A benchmark score is therefore a score for a whole pipeline. It says little about model size on its own.

Most of these models also predict actions in chunks. One forward pass returns several future actions, and the robot executes them before the policy looks again. Chunking keeps inference affordable and tends to smooth motion. The cost is that, for the length of the chunk, the policy is blind to what is happening. How many actions are executed before the next query is usually a deployment setting, not part of the model, and papers often mention it in passing if at all.

The idea is older than the large policies. ACT predicts a chunk of future actions to shorten the effective horizon of fine bimanual tasks and, at run time, asks for a new chunk at every step and blends the overlapping predictions~\cite{zhao2023act}. Diffusion Policy generates action sequences by iterative denoising and executes only the first part of each before predicting again, a receding horizon~\cite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}. The action expert of the $\pi_0$ family is trained with flow matching, a related generative recipe~\cite{lipman2023flow,black2024pi0visionlanguageactionflowmodel}. These three differ most in how much of a chunk they trust. ACT trusts only the first action, in effect. Diffusion Policy trusts a part. A client that, like the one used in this study, executes an entire chunk before looking again (50 actions in Octo's case) trusts all of it, and that choice sits in the evaluation harness, where it is easy to overlook.

Retail makes all of this harder than a roomy tabletop does. The policy has to tell one branded package from its neighbour, place a gripper to within a centimetre or two in a crowded shelf cell, and live with the fact that every error in the base position shifts what the arm can reach. In a multi-step task those small risks multiply.

The RoboBenchMart authors fully fine-tuned four of these models, Octo, SmolVLA, $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, on their demonstrations and released checkpoints for some of them~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. Fine-tuning is the right choice for a domain-adaptation question. It also means each published number belongs to a chain of four parts: pretrained weights, fine-tuning recipe, serving code and evaluation harness. Download a released checkpoint and you inherit the first two. The last two are whatever your own installation happens to provide.

\section{Reproducibility of evaluation results}

That machine-learning results are hard to reproduce is old news. In deep reinforcement learning, Henderson et al. showed that random seeds, implementation details and evaluation choices can shift reported performance by more than the gaps between the methods being compared~\cite{henderson2018deep}. The NeurIPS reproducibility programme answered with checklists and code-submission policies, and was candid that releasing code is necessary but nowhere near sufficient~\cite{pineau2021improving}. Robotics inherits all of this and adds its own complications. Physical trials are expensive to repeat. Simulated ones depend on simulator versions, assets and controller details that keep moving.

Checking released policies in simulation has become a research topic of its own. SimplerEnv builds simulated versions of real evaluation setups and asks whether policies rank the same way in both, paying careful attention to mismatches in control and appearance~\cite{li2024simplerenv}. The broader message I take from it is simple: before a checkpoint and an environment can tell you anything about the policy, you have to be sure they belong together.

Benchmark papers normally report numbers from one evaluation setup, run by the people who wrote the benchmark. Independent re-runs of the same released artefacts are much less common. I suspect part of the reason is that when the numbers disagree, the cause is hard to find after the fact, and an unexplained discrepancy is an awkward thing to publish. A re-evaluation earns its keep by recording exactly what was run, so that any disagreement can later be traced to a version, a setting or a protocol detail instead of being left as a mystery. Surveying 400 papers in artificial intelligence, Gundersen and Kjensmo found that few documented enough of their method, data and experiments to be reproduced~\cite{gundersen2018state}. The lesson I take for my own records is to write down the boring things: revision numbers, seeds, step caps, the exact command line.

Reporting uncertainty is part of the same discipline. Agarwal et al.\ show that point estimates from a handful of runs can hide differences that are statistically indistinguishable, and argue for interval estimates in their place~\cite{agarwal2021precipice}. Colas et al.\ ask the complementary question, how many runs are needed before a difference of a given size can be detected at all~\cite{colas2018seeds}. Both were written about reinforcement learning, where an ``episode'' is cheap and a training run is not. In an evaluation like this one the economics are reversed: training is someone else's problem, and each episode costs between half a minute and three minutes of wall-clock time, depending on the task. That makes the sample size a choice, and this dissertation's choice of 30 episodes per configuration is a limited one (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

\section{Generalisation, metrics and diagnostic evaluation}

``Generalisation'' means little until you say along which axis. Move the robot's starting pose and you test local control. Change a texture and you test visual invariance. Change the floor plan and the approach path and camera geometry change with it. Ask for a product that was demonstrated in a different task and you test whether the policy learned a skill that transfers between objects; ask for one it has never seen and the test is harder again. Fold all of these into a single ``unseen'' score and most of the information disappears.

The RoboBenchMart protocol builds a ladder of this kind. Training-seed evaluation replays the configurations that produced the demonstrations. In-domain evaluation keeps the scenes but randomises where the robot starts. Unseen-scene evaluation changes the store, and unseen-scene-and-item evaluation pairs a familiar skill with a product that was never demonstrated for it. The headline metric is the success rate,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{S}_{m,t,c}=\frac{1}{N_{t,c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t,c}} \mathbb{I}\!\left[\text{episode } i \text{ satisfies the task predicate}\right],
    \label{eq:success-rate}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is a model, $t$ a task, $c$ a condition and $N_{t,c}$ the number of episodes pooled for that task and condition. Upstream uses 100 episodes per task--item--fixture triplet. Even at 100 the uncertainty in each rate is noticeable; at the 30 used here it is large, and an interval says more than a point estimate.

A success rate tells you how often a task gets done. It does not tell you why it fails. For that, the upstream authors define an eleven-category taxonomy that files each failed episode under the earliest stage at which it went wrong. Taking the earliest stage stops one mistake from being counted several times as it cascades, although it also hides whatever damage happened afterwards, and every label is still a human judgement. Applying such a scheme consistently needs per-episode evidence, ideally video and state trajectories together.

\section{Position of this dissertation}

I do not propose a model, a benchmark or a new evaluation condition. I take one existing benchmark and two of its released checkpoints and ask what happens when someone else runs them from what is public, and what that run can and cannot show. The contribution belongs to the reproducibility layer described above: a complete, hashed record of what was run, the repairs needed to run it, and a close reading of what the protocol measures and what it does not. The benchmark itself, including everything described in Chapter~\ref{chap:system}, is the work of the RoboBenchMart authors.

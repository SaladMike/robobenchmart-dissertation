\section{Why benchmark design matters}

A robot benchmark is sometimes treated as a collection of tasks plus a leaderboard. That description misses its scientific role. A useful benchmark specifies what changes, what remains fixed, how success is measured and which conclusions are licensed by the comparison. In robot learning, those choices are unusually consequential because the observation distribution, embodiment, controller and physical reset procedure can all change the apparent difficulty of a task.

Real-world evaluation has the strongest claim to operational relevance, but repeated trials are expensive and difficult to standardise. Objects wear, cameras move, human resetters differ and a failed manipulation can alter the next episode. Simulation trades some physical realism for repeatability, privileged state and rapid reset. It also makes counterfactual evaluation possible: the same policy can be tested with a new floor texture, a different store plan or a changed product split while other variables remain controlled. The trade is worthwhile only if the simulator preserves the geometric and interaction constraints that define the target domain.

General manipulation suites illustrate different points on this design spectrum. RLBench offers language-described tasks in simulation and a broad set of demonstrations~\cite{james2019rlbench}. ALFRED combines instruction following, navigation and interaction in household environments~\cite{ALFRED20}. CALVIN emphasises long-horizon language-conditioned control and sequential task completion~\cite{mees2022calvin}; LIBERO studies transfer and lifelong manipulation~\cite{liu2023libero}; RoboCasa and ManiSkill-derived suites add rich household scenes and scalable physics~\cite{robocasa2024,taomaniskill3,shukla2024maniskillhab}. VLABench and BEHAVIOR-1K broaden task and object variety~\cite{zhang2024vlabench,li2022behavior}, and RoboCAS targets manipulation under complex object arrangement~\cite{zheng2024robocas}. These projects establish that language, multi-task data and controlled evaluation can be brought into one framework. They also reveal a domain bias: kitchens, living rooms and tabletop workspaces are far more common than densely stocked stores.

That bias is not superficial. Retail shelves contain repeated instances, near-duplicate packaging, tight clearances and fragile scene state. Fixtures create narrow aisles and long navigation distances. A policy may need to identify ``the Fanta bottle'' among visually adjacent branded products and then approach it from a pose compatible with the gripper. Household benchmark coverage therefore does not automatically imply retail competence. A dedicated benchmark is justified when the domain changes the distribution of geometry, appearance and failure, not simply when it supplies a new background texture.

\section{Retail datasets and the missing interaction layer}

Retail computer vision has produced substantial resources for recognition and dense detection. SKU110K is designed around crowded shelf images, while RP2K supports fine-grained retail product recognition~\cite{goldman2019precise,peng2020rp2k}. These datasets expose a hard perceptual problem: many products occupy a small fraction of the image and differ through subtle packaging cues. StandardSim adds synthetic retail imagery and controlled visual generation~\cite{mata2022standardsim}. Such resources can train or test perception, yet their labels stop before action. They cannot determine whether a selected instance is reachable, whether the hand collides with a neighbouring item or whether the object remains stable after placement.

Three-dimensional assets narrow part of this gap. Generic repositories such as YCB and ShapeNet have long supported manipulation and shape research~\cite{calli2015ycb,chang2015shapenet}. Retail-specific 3D collections better match product geometry and texture; IPA-3D1K, for example, describes a collection of reconstructed products~\cite{lindermayr2023ipa}. Access, licensing and scale remain practical constraints, while a set of meshes still does not define a store. Assets need consistent units, orientation, collision geometry and materials. They must be placed on support surfaces, grouped like stock and simplified enough that hundreds of instances can be rendered interactively.

Recent work begins to treat shelf picking as a generative robotics problem. FetchBot focuses on highly cluttered shelf arrangements, safety-aware retrieval and zero-shot transfer to a physical robot~\cite{liu2025fetchbot}. That focus is valuable, but an isolated shelf does not test movement through a store, variation in fixture layout or interaction with articulated refrigeration. The benchmark developed in this dissertation is complementary: it trades some task-specific generation depth for a larger end-to-end environment that joins plan generation, product placement, mobile manipulation and policy evaluation.

The closest neighbouring systems make the boundary sharper. FetchBench procedurally generates constrained fetching scenes---shelves, drawers, cabinets and baskets among them---and evaluates the full approach--grasp--retrieve chain with both modular and learned baselines~\cite{pmlr-v270-han25a}. It is deliberately object- and geometry-centred rather than retail-specific. Sari Sandbox moves in the other direction. It provides three virtual grocery-store layouts, more than 250 interactive products, an API-controlled embodied agent and SariBench human demonstrations for shopping tasks~\cite{gajo2025sari}. MarketGen, released later, generates complete supermarket environments from text or reference images, reports more than 1,100 goods and evaluates checkout unloading and in-aisle mobile manipulation with a sim-to-real study~\cite{hu2025marketgen}. \ref{tab:retail-benchmark-comparison} summarises the overlap without forcing unlike systems into a single ranking.

This chronology rules out an unqualified claim to be the ``first open retail benchmark''. FetchBench predates this work but is not a retail-store benchmark; Sari Sandbox already supplies open retail interaction and human demonstration data; MarketGen is later but overlaps strongly in complete-store generation and mobile manipulation. Priority therefore depends on an artificially narrow definition. The defensible contribution is the particular combination studied here: procedurally generated dark-store layouts, a mobile manipulator, VLA adaptation, controlled scene--item distribution shifts and an earliest-cause failure analysis in one reproducible evaluation path.

The literature therefore suggests a layered gap. There is abundant 2D appearance data, a smaller number of 3D retail assets and even fewer systems that turn those assets into reproducible manipulation episodes. The missing layer is interaction: a task API, controller, success predicate and dataset path that let researchers move from ``which product is visible?'' to ``can the robot safely complete the requested operation?''

\section{Procedural environments and structured variation}

Procedural generation is attractive because a finite hand-built scene can be memorised. Randomness alone, however, is not enough. Uniformly scattering fixtures across a floor creates collisions, blocked aisles and arrangements that do not resemble stores. The generator needs a structural prior.

The fixture-generation component draws inspiration from tensor-field methods for procedural street modelling~\cite{chen2008interactive}. In that setting, a field encodes locally preferred orientations; curves follow the field to form coherent road networks. A store presents a related geometric problem. Shelves usually align in rows, follow boundaries and leave passages between them. A second-order tensor is particularly useful because its principal directions are orientation-like: a shelf aligned at $0$ degrees is equivalent to one aligned at $180$ degrees. This symmetry avoids an arbitrary forward direction.

Several alternatives were possible. A fixed catalogue of plans would guarantee plausibility but provide limited continuous variation. Pure rejection sampling would diversify pose but struggle to produce coherent rows. Grammar-based layouts could encode strong design knowledge, although every new fixture relation would expand the grammar. A learned generator might capture examples from real plans but would require an appropriate corpus and a validity layer. The tensor-field approach occupies a practical middle ground. Boundary and obstacle geometry induces local directions, while rejection tests still enforce collision and aisle constraints.

Procedural shelf population raises a different set of constraints. Products sit on detected support surfaces, typically in aligned groups with small pose perturbations. Depletion should create gaps without converting the shelf into physically implausible noise. The implementation here builds on \texttt{scene\_synthesizer}, which provides scene and support-surface operations~\cite{eppner2025scene}. It assigns product groups to regular grid positions. Stacking and stochastic depletion were part of the design and are not part of the implementation; the system chapter says where that line falls. What the arranger does deliver is grid placement under controlled perturbation, which is enough to make arrangement an experimental variable rather than a fixed property of a static scene.

Visual diversity must be accompanied by computational discipline. High-resolution product meshes multiply quickly: a few hundred products per store can dominate memory and rendering time. Hierarchical geometric representations have long used lower-detail models for objects that occupy little screen space~\cite{clark1976hierarchical}. The proposed benchmark applies that principle offline. Several simplification strategies create candidate meshes; candidates are compared using geometric error and triangle reduction; background shelves use simplified assets while the robot's nearby workspace retains detail. This is an engineering optimisation rather than a learning contribution, yet it changes which experiments are feasible.

\section{Generating robot demonstrations}

Robot policy adaptation requires trajectories. Teleoperation gives human-like behaviour and can handle complex contact, but collection time grows with task and scene diversity. Physical teleoperation also carries reset cost. In simulation, privileged object poses and collision models make automatic collection possible. The central trade-off is between scalability and behavioural naturalness.

Sampling-based motion planning supplies collision-aware paths without learning a policy~\cite{LaValle_2006}. RRT-Connect grows trees from the start and goal configurations and has been widely used for high-dimensional planning~\cite{kuffner2000rrt}. Optimisation-based methods such as CHOMP refine trajectories using smoothness and obstacle costs~\cite{ZuckerChomp}; asymptotically optimal sampling methods offer stronger theoretical properties at additional computational cost~\cite{KaramanSamplingBased}. For demonstration generation, the best planner is not necessarily the one with the strongest asymptotic guarantee. It is the one that produces enough successful, sufficiently varied executions under a clear acceptance test.

The demonstration pipeline uses a staged planner. Task knowledge defines anchor poses for approach, grasp, transport and placement. Between anchors, the system first attempts a screw motion for segments that do not require base movement~\cite{murray2017mathematical}. The path is checked for collision. If it is invalid, RRT-Connect is used as a fallback. Base repositioning relies on task-specific heuristics because mobile approach geometry is tightly connected to the fixture. Failed plans are discarded and the environment is reset; only complete successful episodes enter the training set.

This scheme should be interpreted carefully. It is scalable relative to manual demonstration, and its deterministic seeds improve reproducibility. It does not discover task structure automatically. Anchor design transfers human knowledge into the collector, and retaining only successes changes the trajectory distribution. The demonstrations are suited to imitation learning because they show completion, but they do not teach recovery from every error. Methods such as MimicGen take another route, transforming a small number of human source demonstrations across scenes and object poses~\cite{mandlekar2023mimicgen}. Reinforcement learning can generate behaviour through reward optimisation~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,haarnoja2017soft}, yet reward design, exploration and computation remain substantial for long-horizon mobile manipulation. These families are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

\section{Generalist vision-language-action policies}

Large heterogeneous robot datasets have encouraged policies that share representations across tasks and embodiments. Open X-Embodiment and DROID exemplify efforts to aggregate broad real-robot experience~\cite{open_x_embodiment_rt_x_2023,khazatsky2024droid}, addressing a data regime in which no single laboratory can cheaply collect every task and platform. Generalist policies then condition action generation on images, language and robot state.

Octo is a transformer-based generalist policy designed for adaptation across robots and tasks~\cite{octo_2023}. OpenVLA uses a vision-language backbone with autoregressive action prediction~\cite{kim24openvla}, Dita scales a diffusion transformer to the same problem~\cite{hou2025dita}, and the $\pi_0$ family pairs a vision-language foundation with a flow-based action component~\cite{black2024pi0visionlanguageactionflowmodel}. SmolVLA targets a smaller and more accessible model footprint~\cite{shukor2025smolvlavisionlanguageactionmodelaffordable}. $\pi_{0.5}$ extends the $\pi$ family toward open-world generalisation~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld}. These designs differ in scale, pretraining mixtures, action representation and adaptation recipe. A benchmark comparison therefore measures complete model pipelines, not model size in isolation.

Retail evaluation creates four pressures that are less visible in a spacious tabletop setup. Grounding must discriminate branded instances in clutter. Manipulation needs centimetre-level alignment in constrained shelf cells. Mobile-base error changes the arm's reachable set. Finally, task composition multiplies the probability of failure: even a policy with atomic success probability $p$ has an idealised three-stage success of $p^3$ if errors are independent. Real stages are not independent, so the multiplication is only an intuition, but it explains why moderate atomic rates cannot guarantee dependable order completion.

The benchmark fine-tunes four models rather than comparing zero-shot behaviour. This is appropriate for the question of domain adaptation: given a modest retail corpus, how much of the pretrained capability can be specialised, and what transfers beyond the training combinations? It also prevents an unfair interpretation of failure as evidence that the language model has never seen a product name. The comparison still has confounds, and in this study one of them swallows the rest. The adaptation budget available here is a single consumer GPU, which fixes a batch size of eight, short step budgets and low-rank updates in place of full fine-tuning for the larger three---a configuration chosen to fit the card rather than to reproduce any particular published setting. Architecture, parameter count, pretraining mixture and adaptation method therefore vary together across the four rows, and so does how far each was trained from convergence. What follows compares four pipelines under a shared constraint. It does not compare four architectures.

\section{Generalisation, metrics and diagnostic evaluation}

Generalisation is meaningful only after its axis is named. A new robot pose tests local control robustness. A new texture tests visual invariance. A new floor plan changes navigation and camera geometry. An item that appeared in another task but not the current one tests whether the policy learned a transferable skill--object relation. A completely unseen object tests a stronger form of semantic and geometric transfer. Collapsing these cases into a single ``unseen'' score would hide useful structure.

The evaluation protocol consequently defines a progression. In-domain evaluation randomises the initial robot pose. Unseen-scene evaluation additionally changes textures and store layouts. Unseen-scenes-and-items evaluation introduces task--item pairings absent during task-specific training, using items that were encountered elsewhere. The generator also supports unseen shelf arrangements and entirely unseen products, but these conditions are not in the headline comparison because easier cases already produce near-zero performance for several models.

Success rate is the primary metric:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{S}_{m,t,c}=\frac{1}{N_{t,c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t,c}} \mathbb{I}\!\left[\text{episode } i \text{ satisfies the task predicate}\right],
    \label{eq:success-rate}
\end{equation}
where $m$ denotes a model, $t$ a task, $c$ a condition and $N_{t,c}$ the number of trials aggregated for that task and condition. The indicator is one only when the environment-level completion checks pass. The reported protocol uses 100 trials per task--item--fixture triplet. Averaging gives a clear operational result, but it offers limited explanation.

Failure taxonomy supplies the missing diagnostic layer. Each unsuccessful episode is assigned the earliest primary failure in the execution chain: wrong task, wrong target, base movement, pre-grasp, grasp, drop, non-target displacement, pre-place, placement coordinate, release or partial articulation. Choosing the earliest primary failure avoids double-counting cascades. If the robot selects the wrong product and later drops it, the earlier grounding error is more informative for intervention. The label is still a human interpretation, and a single label cannot express interacting causes. The dissertation therefore uses the taxonomy to identify dominant bottlenecks rather than presenting it as an objective physical measurement.

\section{Position of this dissertation}

The reviewed literature shows mature components but an incomplete retail pipeline. Simulation benchmarks contribute reproducibility, retail datasets contribute appearance diversity, motion planners contribute scalable successful trajectories and generalist VLA policies contribute transferable representations. The proposed benchmark joins these elements around a domain-specific evaluation question.

Its principal novelty is thus system and benchmark engineering. The tensor-field layout generator and retail task definitions are domain-specific methods; the controlled shift protocol and failure analysis support empirical insight. The dissertation does not claim a new VLA algorithm, a new universal metric or a theorem about policy generalisation. This more limited position is also more defensible. The benchmark's contribution can be judged by whether it creates valid scenes, reproducible data, meaningful tasks and evidence that was difficult to obtain with existing resources.

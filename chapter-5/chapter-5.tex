\section{Reading the results}

Three kinds of evidence appear in this chapter and they answer different questions.

Scene and simulation measurements say whether the generated stores are usable at all---whether a plan can be built, instantiated and stepped quickly enough to run an experiment on. Demonstration and interface results say whether one adaptation pipeline can be laid across four architectures that share almost nothing internally. Policy success rates and failure labels say what happens when the scene changes underneath a trained policy. Only the third is about the models.

Keeping the three apart matters more than it sounds. A benchmark can be built correctly and still return nothing but low numbers, and those numbers are then a fact about the policies rather than a fault in the instrument. Much of this chapter is spent establishing which of the two a given result belongs to.

\ref{tab:main-results} carries the policy comparison. Values are mean success percentages over the applicable task--item--fixture combinations, with a hundred trials per triplet. A dash marks a condition the door tasks do not have; it is not a zero. The two composite columns are zero for every model and every scenario.

One thing the table deliberately does not do is collapse. Averaging a 95\% door closing against a 14\% shelf pick produces a number, and the number means nothing: the two tasks succeed and fail for unrelated reasons. Read down a column and you see whether a capability is there at all. Read across a row and you see what shift does to it. Read a single overall score and you see neither.

\section{RQ1: scene generation and computational usability}

RQ1 asked for variation without loss of structure, and on the operational half of that question the pipeline delivers. Different seeds give different store plans rather than reshuffles of one room. Each plan serialises to JSON under a configuration hash, instantiates in ManiSkill3 and keeps its aisles open. Fixtures come out in coherent runs---rows of three to five units placed end to end, with free space between them---rather than scattered across the floor, as \ref{fig:store-generation} shows.

That structure deserves a careful sentence, because the mechanism producing it is coarser than the equations in the system chapter on their own suggest. Every shelf is axis-aligned. What the field decides is where one may sit, not which way it points, and with a decay coefficient of 12 that decision falls to whichever wall or fixture contour happens to lie within about thirty centimetres. The rows are real. They come from a gate applied along a sweep, not from continuous alignment to a smoothly blended orientation.

One sentence from the earlier draft of this section has to go entirely. Product placement was described as contributing controlled depletion alongside visual density. It contributes density. Depletion has no counterpart in the implementation and generated no scene in this study---every shelf, in every training and evaluation episode, is fully packed---and the figure that illustrated it has been removed from the system chapter along with the claim. What the generator was designed to do and what the study exercised were being reported as one thing, and they are not the same thing.

Computation is where RQ1 comes out cleanly, and \ref{fig:mesh-performance} says something sharper than a speed-up factor. Fifty simulation steps were timed on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 as the number of stocked shelving units grew, comparing original product meshes against a setting where only the nearest unit keeps full detail.

The simplified series is flat. One unit costs 0.42~seconds, eight costs 0.23, twenty costs 0.41 and 118 costs 0.42---a 118-fold increase in stocked shelving for no measurable increase in cost, with the spread across the four points smaller than the variation between repeats. The original series is not flat, and it does not go far: 0.21~seconds at one unit, 0.53 at two and 1.24 at four, where it stops. There are no measurements beyond four because at full mesh resolution the scene had already become impractical to step.

That is the finding, and it is a harder one than a ratio. Without simplification, this benchmark tops out at roughly four shelving units, which is a corner of a shop rather than a shop. With it, a 118-unit store steps in a third of the time the four-unit original needed. Asset geometry, not the physics solver, decides which experiments are possible here.

Two honest qualifications attach to it. The contrast just drawn pairs the 118-unit simplified scene against the four-unit original, so it moves scene size and mesh detail together and belongs to that pair of settings rather than to the simplification alone. And the simplification is not free at small scale: at one shelving unit the simplified assets are the slower option, 0.42~seconds against 0.21, with the crossover somewhere between two and four units. Since the two series share only their smallest x-values, the figure contains no matched comparison in the range where simplification actually matters. Producing one means running the original meshes at eight units and above, which is precisely what the missing points at the top of that series suggest was not feasible.

A separate throughput measurement varied the number of parallel environments, stepping random actions through a navigation environment and recording aggregate frame rate and device memory. It is worth being clear about what that measures. No policy, no task, no success---simulation and rendering cost and nothing else.

The two observation modes behave differently in kind rather than in degree, and an earlier draft of this section obscured that by reporting diminishing returns in both. Only the rendered mode diminishes. With state observations, aggregate throughput rises close to linearly out to 128 environments while device memory grows by a couple of gigabytes. With RGB observations, it flattens well before sixty-four environments, and memory grows several times faster, because every additional environment adds geometry traversal, shading and an image transfer on top of its physics step. Matched at the same number of environments, rendering costs roughly an order of magnitude in throughput and several times the memory.

Two regimes follow: state-only scenes when the bottleneck is sample count and rendered scenes when the image is the point, as it is for every policy result in this dissertation. In that second regime, a sixteen-gigabyte card is the binding constraint well before the simulator is.

The underlying numbers are not reproduced here. They were recorded during the study but the result files were not retained alongside the figure, and reporting digitised values would give them a precision they no longer have. The qualitative contrast is robust to that; the exact frame rates are not, and anyone planning capacity should rerun the sweep on their own hardware, which the measurement procedure supports directly.

So RQ1 is answered yes on generation and yes on cost. On the rest of its own wording, it is answered less comfortably, and the wording is worth going back to: scenes that are collision-free, navigable and visually plausible.

Collision-free holds by construction, since a candidate that fails the footprint or passage test is never placed. Visually plausible was never measured. No realism study, no comparison against a distribution of surveyed store plans, no physical validation---the scenes look like stores to me, and that is the whole of the evidence. Navigable holds geometrically and was never exercised: the plans leave two metres of passage and support base motion, and not one episode in this study required a robot to use it, because every episode begins 1.4~metres from the fixture it needs. Section~\ref{sec:verification} takes that up properly.

A generator can therefore be judged sound on the criteria it was built against while leaving the most interesting of those criteria untested. That is the position here.

\section{RQ2: demonstration generation and adaptation}

The collection covers twelve task--item--fixture triplets: 248 successful episodes apiece, 2,976 trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions. It took about ten hours of wall-clock time on a single RTX 4090. Anchor decomposition with screw motion and an RRT-Connect fallback reached that without anyone touching a teleoperation rig, and one observation--action schema then fed four model families whose internals have almost nothing in common. As a supervision source, the pipeline does what it was built to do.

Two things about the corpus are thinner than the totals imply. The layout pool behind it is smaller than the documentation states, because two configuration files meant to generate different stores for board transfer were given the same seed, as were the two for floor recovery; those tasks draw on five distinct plans rather than ten. And the fallback structure is less redundant than the methodology chapter describes---the second screw attempt repeats the first call with identical arguments, which for a deterministic solver cannot change anything. Neither issue costs a result. Both narrow what ``scalable and diverse'' is entitled to mean here.

The more revealing comparison sits in the appendix rather than the main table, and it is the one between Seeds and Pose. Nothing about the scene changes between those two columns. The store is the same store; the shelf holds the same products in the same places; and the instruction is word for word what it was during collection. All that moves is where the robot happens to be standing when the episode starts. The bounds differ by task and neither is large: roughly twenty centimetres of retreat and a quarter of a metre sideways for basket picking, a little under half a metre on both axes for board transfer, with about twenty-two degrees of yaw in either case.

Octo goes from 5\%, 3\% and 3\% on its three board-transfer products to 3\%, 1\% and 1\%. $\pi_{0.5}$ goes from 2\%, 2\% and 2\% on pick-to-basket to 1\%, 1\% and 1\%.

The direction is the one the comparison was built to expose, and the magnitude is too small to build on. Two episodes in a hundred is what separates Octo's best board-transfer product from its retreat under a quarter-metre change of stance. Under a training budget that produced measurable rates, this comparison would be the sharpest instrument in the chapter, because it needs no argument about what counts as a distribution shift---nothing about the scene changes at all. Under this one it is a column of single-digit counts moving by one or two, and it is reported here as a measurement that the protocol supports rather than as a finding the data can carry.

SmolVLA is the one row where the shape survives its own smallness. It records zero on every reported pick-and-place product, in every condition, including the training seeds, while still opening and closing refrigerated and showcase fixtures at rates between 1\% and 4\%. A model that never once places a product in the basket it was shown 248 times, but does occasionally move a door, is not failing uniformly. Whether that pattern would persist under a fuller adaptation budget is exactly what this study cannot establish.

I cannot say what happened, and under the present budget the list of candidates is longer rather than shorter. Capacity, pretraining mixture, action normalisation, low-rank adaptation and a step budget one to two orders of magnitude below the published recipe are all available explanations, and this experiment separates none of them. A near-zero row is the point at which a benchmark stops being informative about a model, and by that standard the table is largely uninformative about all four.

RQ2 divides cleanly, and the two halves deserve separate answers. On demonstration generation it is answered yes: the pipeline produced 2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions from task specifications without a teleoperation rig, and a single observation--action schema fed four architectures with almost nothing in common. On adaptation it is answered only in the weakest sense available. The corpus was sufficient to elicit retail behaviour that is not zero---111 of the 144 per-item cells record at least one success---and insufficient, at this budget, to produce dependable behaviour in any model. Which of those two constraints was binding, the corpus or the compute, this study cannot separate.

Part of the reason is visible in what the collector keeps. It keeps successes, and only successes. The policy learns what a nominal planned trajectory looks like; it receives less evidence about how to correct a base error or reattempt a missed grasp. The prevalence of pre-grasp and grasp failures is consistent with this limitation, although the present experiments do not prove a causal link.

They also do not rule out a competing account, which Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes} returns to. The evaluation client requests a fifty-step action chunk and executes all of it before constructing the next observation, so a policy sees the scene between ten and twenty times across a whole episode and spends the intervening steps committed to a plan it cannot revise. Missing recovery data and missing opportunity to recover produce the same failure label.

\section{RQ3: atomic-task performance}
\label{sec:atomic}

No model learns these tasks under the training budget available here. That sentence is the result, and everything else in this section is an argument about what may and may not be read into it.

The whole of \ref{tab:per-item}---four models, three conditions, twelve task--item--fixture combinations, 144 cells---spans zero to nine successes in a hundred trials, with a mean of 1.5. The single best cell in the study is Octo closing a showcase door at 9\%. Under the Pose condition of \ref{tab:main-results}, no model exceeds 4\% on any task. A hundred and eleven of the 144 cells are non-zero, so the policies are not inert: they occasionally complete a retail manipulation, and the environment registers it when they do. They simply do not do so often enough for any of the comparisons this chapter was designed to make.

The reason is stated in Section~\ref{sec:training} and is not in doubt. Every model was adapted on one RTX 4090 at batch size 8, for between 30,000 and 100,000 steps. The published recipes for these policies use eight-GPU clusters, batch sizes between 64 and 256, and step budgets one to two orders of magnitude larger. Three of the four were adapted through low-rank updates rather than full fine-tuning. What \ref{tab:main-results} measures is what these architectures do after a few hours of single-card adaptation, which is a legitimate question and a different one from how well they can learn retail manipulation.

The consequence for interpretation is severe and worth stating in arithmetic rather than in hedging. At a hundred trials per cell, a 1\% entry is one successful episode and a 4\% entry is four. The Wilson interval for one success in a hundred runs from 0.2\% to 5.4\%; for four successes it runs from 1.6\% to 9.8\%. Every pair of cells in \ref{tab:main-results} has overlapping intervals. No statement of the form ``model A is better than model B'' or ``task X is harder than task Y'' survives that, and this chapter makes none.

Three claims a reader may expect from the surrounding literature are worth checking against this table and finding absent. That articulated doors are easier than shelf picking: not supported here---door and pick-and-place columns are separated by two or three episodes. That floor recovery is anomalously hard: not supported---it sits at the floor along with everything else, and under the Seeds condition Octo's highest task mean is in fact floor recovery at 7.5\%. That one policy family transfers better than another: not supported, and Section~\ref{sec:results-shift} explains why the shift comparison cannot be run at all from this baseline. None of the three is refuted by these results; each is simply beyond what they can reach.

One asymmetry is large enough to note without resting anything on it. Octo, the smallest model at 93M parameters and the only one fully fine-tuned rather than adapted through low-rank updates, produces the highest numbers in the table. Whether that reflects the architecture, the smaller parameter count fitting the budget better, or full fine-tuning being the more effective use of a fixed number of steps, this experiment cannot say, since adaptation method and model scale move together across the four rows. It is recorded here as an observation with an experiment attached rather than as a finding.

\section{Distribution shift}
\label{sec:results-shift}

The shift experiment cannot be evaluated from this baseline, and saying so is more useful than reporting the numbers as though it could.

Measuring degradation requires something to degrade. Across the twenty model--task pairs of \ref{tab:main-results}, the change from Pose to Layout is zero percentage points in fifteen of them and one point in four. The largest single movement anywhere is Octo gaining three points on door closing, from 4\% to 7\%---a change of three episodes in a hundred, in the direction opposite to the one a shift is supposed to produce. Whatever a new store plan and new surface materials do to these policies, it is smaller than the noise of the measurement, because the policies are already at the floor before the store changes.

The distinction between an absent effect and an unmeasurable one matters here, and the two are easy to confuse. A study reporting in-domain rates of forty or fifty percent could report a twenty-point loss under a new store plan and mean something by it. Here the in-domain rates are between zero and four percent, and a policy that cannot perform a task in the store it trained on cannot be observed failing to transfer that performance to another one. The benchmark's shift conditions are intact and correctly constructed; the experiment simply has no signal to put through them.

Pairing is the one condition where something is still visible, and only because it is bounded below. Octo and SmolVLA record zero on all three applicable tasks. $\pi_0$ retains a single success on floor recovery. $\pi_{0.5}$ records 1\% on pick-to-basket, 1\% on floor recovery and 3\% on board transfer, which are its highest figures under any condition---an ordering that is almost certainly an artefact of small counts rather than evidence that the hardest condition is easier. What can be said is that the two smaller policies reach exact zero when the target product is one they were never asked to handle on that task, and that this is consistent with, though not evidence for, a grounding failure.

The attribution that was available in the earlier version is no longer available here, and its absence should be recorded rather than replaced. Whether current generalist policies transfer across retail-specific geometric and semantic shift is a question this study is now unable to answer in either direction. It established the conditions, generated the scenes, ran the protocol, and found the policies too weakly adapted for the conditions to discriminate between them. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} sets out what a budget capable of answering it would need to look like.

One property of the setup remains worth stating because it bounds the question independently of the training budget. It is tempting to explain any Layout effect partly through changed navigation geometry, since the layout moves and the aisles move with it. Episode initialisation does not permit that reading. The robot is placed 1.4~metres in front of the correct fixture and turned to face it at the start of every episode, in every condition, with randomisation on the order of a quarter of a metre and twenty-two degrees applied on top. Nothing in the reported protocol asks a policy to cross a store or to choose between distant fixtures. Even with a budget sufficient to produce measurable rates, what varies between Pose and Layout would be the visual context and the final metre of approach, and no more than that.

\section{Composite tasks}

Every composite-task entry is zero. This is the clearest result in the table and the easiest to overstate. The evaluation does not show that a model cannot understand a multi-step natural-language request, because an oracle supplies atomic instructions. It shows that none of the tested adapted policies completed the full execution chain under the protocol.

Compounding atomic error provides a strong baseline explanation. If three stages each succeeded with probability 0.5 and were independent, complete success would be 12.5\%. With a 0.2 atomic rate, it would be 0.8\%. The actual stages are dependent: a poor placement may alter camera views or block the next approach, and the robot state after one completion differs from a clean reset. Zero observed success is therefore compatible with the atomic rates without requiring a separate language-planning failure.

The finding still matters operationally. Retail value comes from finished orders, not isolated benchmark primitives. A policy that occasionally picks one item but cannot sustain a short sequence is not ready for unattended fulfilment.

A column of zeros is also a poor way to report it, and the reporting is the part that was avoidable. Each composite environment already returns a per-subtask completion flag and a normalised count of how many subtasks the episode reached, alongside the binary success value that became the column above. That information distinguishes a policy failing on its first pick from one failing at the final release. It was computed during evaluation and dropped at the aggregation stage, so recovering it costs no new simulation, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} treats the omission as an error in this dissertation rather than a gap in the benchmark.

\section{Failure modes}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

\ref{tab:failure-share} aggregates the earliest primary labels across the 2,906 annotated failures. For Octo, $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, grasping is the largest category at 30\%, 30\% and 40\%. SmolVLA differs: pre-grasp positioning leads at 40\%, followed by wrong-target interaction at 20\% and grasp at 17\%.

This table is the one place in the chapter where the training budget does not erase the signal, and the reason is structural. Success rates measure how often a policy finishes; failure labels measure where it stops. A policy that finishes one episode in a hundred still produces ninety-nine ordered accounts of where the attempt broke down, and those accounts remain informative about the interaction between the policy and the environment even when the success column does not discriminate. Read the rest of this section on that footing: it describes what these four models do with a retail shelf under a constrained adaptation budget, not how capable they would be with a fuller one.

Target grounding is the second-largest category for all four models and stays between 18\% and 22\% across them. The benchmark therefore exposes a coupled perception--action problem. A policy can generate plausible movement while acting on the wrong branded product. Since the success predicate checks the named item, visually convincing but semantically incorrect manipulation is rejected.

Movement and pre-grasp labels distinguish two spatial scales. SmolVLA's 12\% movement share and 40\% pre-grasp share indicate breakdown before contact. Octo records 10\% and 10\%, while $\pi_{0.5}$ records 1\% and 12\%. The $\pi$ models more often reach interaction, but reaching the object does not solve the task: 40\% of $\pi_{0.5}$'s labelled failures occur at grasp.

Post-grasp side effects expose a trade. $\pi_{0.5}$ has a 7\% displacement share, higher than the other policies. A model that reaches and interacts more often also creates more opportunities to disturb neighbouring items. Strict side-effect checking is therefore important; otherwise an aggressive shelf sweep could appear as progress.

Before that bottleneck is attributed to the policies, two properties of the evaluation setup deserve to sit next to it, because both bear directly on grasping and neither was visible from the results alone.

The first is the execution horizon. A policy is queried once and returns fifty actions; those fifty actions are executed before it observes anything again. Two fifths of $\pi_{0.5}$'s labelled failures occur at the moment of grasp, after it has already reached the correct product---and a wrist that closes two centimetres off target cannot be corrected until the chunk has run out, whatever the model has learned about correcting it. The demonstration corpus and the execution horizon are two different explanations for the same label, and the experiment reported here does not separate them.

The second is the torso. It does not move. Episode initialisation places the prismatic joint at its lowest position and the evaluation client zeroes the corresponding action element before every step, so vertical reach is fixed for the whole episode in every task. That is worth holding against floor recovery in particular: retrieving an object from the ground and returning it to a shelf above waist height, with a body that cannot rise, is a harder problem than the task name suggests, and it applies to every reported floor-recovery episode. Section~\ref{sec:verification} works through both properties and what they cost.

The direction for the next method follows from the shape of all this rather than from any single number. It calls for stronger item-level grounding, contact-aware action adaptation and demonstrations that show recovery. Scaling language capacity alone leaves an unstable gripper pose exactly where it was.

\section{Independent verification of the experimental protocol}

This is the one check an author cannot perform reliably alone: I knew what the system was supposed to do, and that knowledge is exactly what makes it hard to see what it actually does.

The review came back with a list of possible discrepancies. I reproduced each one myself against the code and the stored configurations before accepting it. Everything reported in this section is something I confirmed; anything I could not confirm is marked as such below rather than presented as fact.

A second group of items changes no number in this chapter but does change what the system can be said to do. The tensor field admits or rejects axis-aligned placements rather than orienting shelves continuously. The action vector carries thirteen elements rather than eleven. Stock depletion and vertical stacking were designed but were not used to generate any scene in this study. These are corrected in the system chapter and listed here only so that the correction is visible from the results.

One item resisted confirmation and is reported as unresolved. The evaluation server for Octo runs with a history length of one, while the training configuration in Appendix~\ref{app:training-results} records two history observations. I was not able to establish which setting produced the reported numbers, so Octo's figures should be read as a lower bound on what that checkpoint can do, and no conclusion in this dissertation rests on the difference.

Line the two directions up and the shape of the finding becomes clear. Almost everything that diverged on the perception and generalisation axes diverged toward leniency: the unseen products were visible throughout training, the unseen scenes for half the task suite were the training scenes, the robot began each episode aimed at the right fixture from close range, the success box was generous and the collateral check partial. Had the experiment run exactly as the system and methodology chapters describe, every one of those conditions would have been harder.

The scores would have gone down. They were already near zero across the strongest shift for three of four models and zero everywhere on both composite tasks.

A negative result obtained under conditions more forgiving than intended is a stronger negative result, not a weaker one, and the central claim of this chapter survives the verification with more room to spare than the original design would have given it. What does not survive is part of the diagnostic layer, and that is the real cost. Two of the more quotable interpretations offered above rested on comparisons the experiment did not actually run. Articulated fixtures may or may not be more robust to shift, since the door tasks were never subjected to the same shift. Grasp may dominate the failure distribution because planner-shaped demonstrations teach no recovery or because a policy blind for fifty steps has no opportunity to recover whatever it learned. Both now stand as hypotheses with experiments attached rather than as findings.

The verification also produced one straightforwardly useful correction. The composite evaluation already records a per-subtask completion flag and a normalised count of subtasks reached, alongside the binary success value that became the column of zeros in \ref{tab:main-results}. The information needed to distinguish a policy that fails on its first pick from one that reaches the final release was computed and then discarded at the reporting stage. Recovering it requires no new simulation.

\section{What the benchmark demonstrates}

The instrument and the policy study have to be assessed separately here, because only one of them returned what it was built to return.

The instrument works. Store plans are generated with structured variation and instantiate in ManiSkill3 with aisles open and fixtures collision-free. Asset simplification moves the practical ceiling from four shelving units to a hundred and eighteen, which is what makes a store-scale scene steppable at all. The demonstration collector turned task specifications into 2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions without a teleoperation rig. One observation--action schema fed four policy families with almost nothing in common internally, and the task layer registered success, side effects and articulation state throughout. Every component the first two research questions asked for was built and exercised.

The policy study does not support the comparison it was designed to make. Under adaptation on a single RTX 4090, no model exceeds 4\% on any task in the Pose condition, the whole per-item table spans zero to nine successes in a hundred, and the confidence intervals of every pair of cells overlap. The shift conditions cannot discriminate because there is no in-domain performance for them to degrade. What the table establishes is that these four architectures, adapted for a few hours on one consumer card, do not learn retail mobile manipulation---and that is a statement about the budget at least as much as about the architectures.

Two things nevertheless survive the collapse of the success column, and they are worth separating from the rest because they do not depend on it. The first is the failure distribution. A policy that finishes one episode in a hundred still produces ninety-nine ordered accounts of where the attempt broke down, and \ref{tab:failure-share} shows those accounts differing systematically between models: SmolVLA stops before contact, the $\pi$ models reach the object and fail at grasp. The second is the pair of protocol properties in Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes}---a fifty-step blind execution horizon and a torso pinned at its lowest position for every episode of every task. Both bear directly on grasping, both are independent of how long a model was trained, and both would still be there under any budget.

What this chapter cannot say is whether current generalist policies transfer across retail-specific shift. That question was the point of the study, and it remains open in both directions. The conditions exist, the scenes generate, the protocol runs; the policies were too weakly adapted for the measurement to mean anything. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} begins with the experiment that would change that.

One qualification governs everything in this section. The protocol that produced these numbers is not in every respect the one the system and methodology chapters describe, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} sets out the confirmed differences. Several conditions turned out to be more forgiving than intended---unseen items that were visible throughout training, unseen scenes that were the training scenes for half the task suite, a robot that always began aimed at the right fixture---and a smaller number turned out to be harsher. Under the present results that accounting matters less than it once did: a test made more permissive than described still returned near-zero rates, which strengthens the reading that the binding constraint was the adaptation budget rather than the difficulty of the conditions.

\section{Implications and a targeted ablation programme}
\label{sec:ablations}

The results suggest a sequence of experiments more useful than immediately adding another model to the leaderboard.

One of them precedes everything else. Until at least one policy is adapted at a budget approaching its published recipe, no other ablation in this section can be read, because each of them measures a difference between conditions and there is currently no performance for a condition to change. The cheapest version is not a cluster: it is one model, chosen for fitting a single card comfortably---SmolVLA at 450M is the obvious candidate---trained until its in-domain success rate stops improving rather than until a fixed step count expires, then evaluated under the existing Seeds, Pose, Layout and Pairing conditions. If its Pose rate reaches even twenty percent, every comparison this chapter had to withdraw becomes measurable again on the scenes and protocol already built. If it does not, the difficulty attribution shifts from the budget to the tasks, and that too would be a result.

Two further experiments come directly out of Section~\ref{sec:verification} and should be run alongside it, because until they are, parts of \ref{tab:main-results} cannot be interpreted at all. One is a matched shift for the door tasks: regenerate their evaluation scenes from a seed family disjoint from training, as the pick-and-place tasks already do, and re-measure the Pose-to-Layout loss. At present the door tasks are evaluated on their own training scenes with only texture and starting pose varied, so their Layout column measures something narrower than the pick-and-place columns do. That has to be repaired whatever the training budget, since it is a defect in the protocol rather than in the result. The other is a sweep over the execution horizon. Re-evaluating an unchanged checkpoint at horizons of one, five, ten and fifty costs no retraining, and if grasp failures fall as the horizon shortens then a substantial share of what the taxonomy currently attributes to contact precision belongs to open-loop execution instead.

The remaining ablations concern the policies rather than the instrument. The first should isolate vision from geometry. Policies could be evaluated on identical layouts with textures changed, identical textures with layouts changed and identical scenes with product pose changed. The present Layout condition combines texture and layout, so any loss it produces cannot be assigned to appearance or to approach geometry. Factorial evaluation would reveal whether augmentation should focus on image statistics, whole-body approach geometry or both.

A second ablation should vary the number and diversity of demonstrations. The configuration used here fixes 248 trajectories per triplet. Training subsets at several sizes would show whether performance is data-limited or whether additional planner-shaped trajectories saturate. Diversity can be changed independently by widening anchor perturbations, varying shelf occupancy and adding more layouts. If twice as many nearly identical successes produce little gain while a smaller diverse set helps, collection effort should shift from volume to coverage.

Recovery data is the next priority. A controlled dataset could perturb the base before pre-grasp, offset the wrist near contact, weaken a grasp or place the object at the edge of the target region. Demonstrations would then show corrective behaviour. Evaluation should measure recovery probability conditional on the perturbation rather than only final task success. This directly tests the hypothesis raised by the failure distribution: policies may know the nominal sequence yet lack a route back after small errors.

Grounding and control should also be separated. One experiment can provide an oracle target mask or 3D target pose while leaving action generation unchanged. If wrong-target errors disappear but grasp remains dominant, perception was only part of the problem. The complementary experiment can retain the policy's target selection but hand the selected object to a privileged grasp planner. The gap between these conditions would quantify the contribution of manipulation precision. Neither oracle represents deployment; both are diagnostic interventions.

Door tasks would make a useful calibration family once any policy performs them reliably enough to compare. Their instruction never names a product, which removes grounding from the picture and leaves articulation and contact as the variables, so controlled changes in handle shape, door mass, friction and required opening distance would map onto performance more directly than they can in the pick-and-place families. Whether closing is genuinely easier than opening---plausible, since closing needs contact while opening needs a handle acquired and held through the swing---is a question the present rates cannot settle.

Composite tasks need richer outputs. Every episode should report the number of completed subtasks, the state at each hand-off and conditional success from stage $k$ to $k+1$. Reset-free continuation can then be compared with an artificial clean reset between atomic stages. If clean-reset composition succeeds while continuous composition does not, state drift and observation change are central. If both remain zero, atomic reliability or instruction switching is sufficient to explain the failure.

Retail-specific pretraining is a plausible response, but it needs a fair test. One model architecture should be pretrained on matched quantities of general manipulation data, synthetic retail data and a mixture. Fine-tuning and evaluation would remain fixed. Such a study could determine whether the retail domain contributes beyond dataset size. The current cross-model table cannot do this because architecture, scale and pretraining mixture all vary together.

Finally, success must be connected to safety and throughput. A policy that completes 60\% of picks but displaces stock in many failures may be less useful than a slower conservative policy. Future reporting could include time to completion, collision impulse, non-target displacement magnitude, recovery count and energy or path length. These are not substitutes for task success. They describe the cost of obtaining it.

This ablation programme follows directly from the benchmark evidence. It treats low scores as a map of unanswered questions, not a verdict that larger models will inevitably solve or fail at retail. The benchmark is most valuable when it narrows the next experiment.

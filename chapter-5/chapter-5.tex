\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}
\begingroup
\justifying
\setlength{\parindent}{2em}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.55\baselineskip}

This chapter reports what happened when the two released checkpoints were run through the 42 atomic configurations described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. I begin with the plainest question, whether the protocol could be executed at all, because every later number depends on it. The success counts follow, first for the three pick-and-place families and then for the door tasks, where nearly all of the successes are found. The second half of the chapter is about interpretation: how far these counts sit from the upstream report, which explanations remain open, and which properties of the protocol limit what can be concluded.

\section{Execution and the evidence that remains}
\label{sec:execution}

All 42 configurations were completed for both models, 30 episodes each. That gives 1,260 episodes per checkpoint and 2,520 in total. The route to that point was not clean, and I record it here because the history affects how the episodes can be compared.

The Octo run started as a single serial batch. It crashed at configuration~8, the first floor task, with \texttt{NameError: get\_actor\_obb}: an upstream commit had removed the import from \texttt{dsynth/envs/pick\_from\_floor.py} while the function was still in use, so the floor environments could not be constructed at all. After I restored the import, the serial batch was resumed from configuration~8. The resumed process stopped after nine further configurations for a reason I was unable to establish; the log ends and no exit file was written. A second resumption completed one more configuration and was then stopped on purpose so that the remaining 25 configurations could run in parallel on four independent Octo servers (ports 8000--8003). Only configurations that finished all 30 episodes enter the results; partial directories left by the interrupted batches were discarded. The $\pi_{0.5}$ run was simpler. Four client processes shared one OpenPI policy server, and each of the 42 configurations wrote its own log with exactly one \texttt{Results:} line.

Two further repairs were needed before the client and servers would work together. The evaluation client read the language instruction from the wrapped environment, which fails once gymnasium wrappers are applied; I changed it to read \texttt{env.unwrapped.language\_instructions}. The Octo server originally shared one policy object, including its observation-history buffer, across every connection; with parallel clients this would have mixed observations from different episodes, so I changed it to construct a separate policy per connection. Neither change touches the success predicates, the scenes or the seeds.

What survives from the two runs is narrower than a full trajectory archive, and the gap is easy to overlook. Each configuration directory holds one MP4 per episode, an HDF5 file and a JSON file. The JSON files list no episodes and every HDF5 file is 800 bytes. The reason is mundane: the evaluation script does not pass \texttt{--save-traj}, and the client calls \texttt{flush\_trajectory()} only when that flag is present. Per-episode outcomes therefore come from the logs, where each \texttt{Results:} line lists the seeds of the successful episodes. I mapped these onto the 2,520 video files and recorded SHA-256 hashes for every artefact. The videos are stored in Git LFS and nobody has watched them systematically, me included. Nothing in this chapter rests on what a video shows.

Against RQ1, then, the answer is yes, with qualifications. The upstream release can be run end to end from public code, scenes and checkpoints, but only after one import fix in an environment file and two interface fixes in the serving path. The evidence that results is a per-episode success flag tied to a seed, a video and a hash. It is not a per-step record of states and actions.

\section{Pick-and-place: 1,800 episodes without a success}
\label{sec:pick-zero}

Neither checkpoint completed a single Basket, Floor or Board episode. That is 900 episodes per model across Seeds, Pose, Layout and Pairing, and 1,800 in all.

A zero from 30 or 60 trials is a weaker statement than it looks, so it helps to say how weak. With the Wilson interval, 0/90 is compatible with a true rate of up to about 4.1\%, 0/60 with up to 6.0\%, and a single 0/30 configuration with up to 11.4\%. Pooled over a model's 900 pick-and-place episodes, the upper bound falls to roughly 0.4\%, although pooling across three task families is only reasonable as a statement about the whole block, not about any one family. What the data rule out is a moderate success rate. They cannot distinguish a checkpoint that never succeeds from one that succeeds a few times in a hundred.

The logs add one piece of information. For every pick-and-place configuration of both models, the mean and maximum episode lengths recorded by the progress bar equal the step cap: 750 for Board and Floor, 600 for Basket. No episode ended early. Beyond that I can say very little about how these episodes failed. There is no per-step trajectory to inspect, no automatic stage-level signal in the logs, and no annotation of the videos. Whether the robot never reached the target, grasped it and dropped it, or disturbed neighbouring stock is unknown, and I have not tried to guess.

For $\pi_{0.5}$ the difference between closing and opening is large. Pooled over Seeds, Pose and Layout, it closed a door in 37 of 180 episodes (20.6\%, Wilson $[15.3, 27.0]$\%) and opened one in 2 of 180 (1.1\%, $[0.3, 4.0]$\%). A two-sided Fisher exact test gives $p \approx 4\times10^{-10}$. With 30 episodes per configuration this is the one contrast in the data that I would call clear.

Why closing should be so much easier I cannot say from these records. It is tempting to argue that a closing door can be pushed with any part of the arm while opening needs a grasp on a handle, and that may well be true, but nothing I recorded distinguishes between that and other explanations. The successful closing episodes are also shorter on average. For $\pi_{0.5}$ the mean recorded length of \texttt{close\_fridge} configurations was 437--452 steps against a cap of 500, and of \texttt{close\_showcase} configurations 827--927 against a cap of 1,000. That fits episodes ending at the moment the predicate is met, and says nothing further about how the door was moved.

Comparing fridge with showcase is less useful than it first appears. The two fixtures have different success predicates: the fridge lid must come within 0.1~rad of its target angle, and a showcase door within 0.2~rad. They also start from different angles and the robot is placed differently in front of them. $\pi_{0.5}$ closed the fridge in 21/90 episodes and a showcase door in 16/90 (Fisher $p \approx 0.46$). Given the different predicates, I do not read this as a ranking of the two fixtures.

\section{Conditions}
\label{sec:conditions}

The four conditions were designed as a ladder of increasing distribution shift. In this run, the ladder can only be read on one rung of one task family. For $\pi_{0.5}$ on Close the counts go 15/60, 10/60 and 12/60 across Seeds, Pose and Layout. The pairwise Fisher tests give $p \approx 0.37$ (Seeds against Pose), $0.81$ (Pose against Layout) and $0.66$ (Seeds against Layout). None of these differences is distinguishable from sampling variation at this sample size. A real degradation of five or ten points could easily be hidden in them.

Even if the differences were larger, two properties of the protocol would limit what they mean. For the door tasks, the Layout configurations load the same scene directories as Seeds and Pose; only the episode seeds (from 42,000) and the initial-pose seeds (from 10,000) change. Door Layout is therefore not a test of transfer to new store geometry. And Pairing, which asks for a familiar skill on a product not demonstrated for that task, was only defined for pick-and-place. Since pick-and-place produced no successes under any condition, Pairing had nothing to degrade from. The condition ran, but it tested nothing in this run.

The Seeds-against-Pose comparison can be made a little sharper. Both conditions use the same 30 episode seeds per configuration; Pose differs only by drawing the robot's starting pose from a separate seed stream. For $\pi_{0.5}$, four \texttt{close\_fridge} seeds and one \texttt{close\_showcase} seed succeeded under both conditions. The overlap is small, which is consistent with the starting pose mattering, but with counts this low I would not build an argument on it.

\section{The two checkpoints}
\label{sec:models}

$\pi_{0.5}$ recorded 39 successes and Octo 4. Because both models were run on the same episode seeds in all 42 configurations (the seeds in the logs and the video file names are identical), the door outcomes can be compared seed by seed. Of the episodes in which at least one model succeeded, both succeeded on 3, only $\pi_{0.5}$ on 36, and only Octo on 1 (\texttt{close\_showcase}, Seeds, seed 104). Treated as matched pairs, a two-sided sign test on 36 against 1 gives $p \approx 5.5\times10^{-10}$.

I report that test as a description, not a formal paired analysis. The pairing is by seed only. The two runs were executed in different ways (Octo partly serial and partly on four independent servers, $\pi_{0.5}$ on four clients sharing one server), on different days, and nothing guarantees that the simulator reached identical states for the same seed under both. The unpaired comparison of Close counts, 37/180 against 4/180, leads to the same conclusion (Fisher $p \approx 2\times10^{-8}$), so the result does not depend on the pairing assumption.

What the comparison licenses is narrow. These two released checkpoints, served the way I served them, on the current code and scenes, differ in how often they close doors. It does not license a statement about the two architectures. The checkpoints differ in pretraining, model size, action head, training steps and batch size; their serving stacks differ; and the protocol was run once. Any of these could carry the difference.

The gap is not a matter of sampling noise. Take the cleanest case, $\pi_{0.5}$ on Basket under Seeds. Upstream reports 82--89\% across the three products. If the true rate in my setting were even a tenth of that, the probability of 0 successes in 90 episodes would be about $(1-0.08)^{90}\approx 5\times10^{-4}$; at the reported rate it is effectively zero. The same reasoning applies to every pick-and-place cell where upstream reports more than a few percent. Something differs systematically between the two evaluations.

What differs, I cannot determine from the records I have. Several candidates are open, and I list them in roughly the order in which I would test them. None has been tested.

\textbf{Environment drift after the checkpoints were released.} The Octo checkpoint I used is dated 4~November 2025 and the $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint 5~November 2025. The upstream code changed afterwards in ways that touch exactly what a policy sees and starts from. A commit on 5~May 2026 (\texttt{1a2a12b}, ``updated initial qpos'') changed the initial joint configuration in \texttt{DarkstoreContinuousBaseEnv}, which the door environments inherit, and in the basket environment, from which Board and Floor inherit; the initial torso height went from 0.36 (0.34 in the basket environment) to 0.0, and the initial arm pose changed as well. Lighting was changed in November 2025 and again in January 2026, motion-planning and robot-model code in April and May 2026, and the demonstration data were regenerated in January 2026. The scene release I evaluated on (\texttt{3dabfaaf}, 22~January 2026) is described as ``new MP data with regen layouts''. The Seeds condition reads its episode seeds from the demonstration files in that release, so it is not guaranteed to reproduce the scenes the checkpoints were trained on, let alone the starting poses or lighting. If the policies are sensitive to their initial arm and torso configuration, this alone could explain much of the gap.

\textbf{Interface mismatch.} The upstream paper describes an 11-dimensional action (seven arm joints, gripper, torso lift and two base velocities). The current client and the OpenPI adapter work with 13 dimensions and overwrite two of them, which by my reading of the controller order are the two head joints (Section~\ref{sec:control}). Thirteen minus two is eleven, which suggests the two descriptions are consistent, but I have not verified that the checkpoints' output dimensions map onto the controller in the order I assume. Normalisation statistics are a related risk; the $\pi_{0.5}$ server loaded them from the checkpoint directory, and I did not compare them with those used in training.

\textbf{Serving differences.} The upstream paper states two frames of observation history for Octo; the released server, and the upstream README, use one. The $\pi_{0.5}$ adapter does not set an action horizon, so it inherits the OpenPI default, which I have not checked on the server revision used.

\textbf{Sample size and run-to-run variation.} Thirty episodes per configuration widen every interval, and I ran the protocol once. This can explain differences of a few points. It cannot explain 84\% against 0\%.

The door results are worth a second look in this light. They are the only cells where my numbers are clearly above zero, and also the cells with the most tolerant predicates and, for closing, possibly the least sensitivity to the arm's starting configuration. That pattern is what one would expect if a systematic shift had damaged fine manipulation while leaving coarse pushing partly intact. I find this suggestive. It is not evidence, and a version-aligned re-evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:future}) is the direct way to find out.

One correction to earlier drafts of this work belongs here. I previously described the torso as fixed at zero. That was wrong. The client overwrites indices 8 and 9 of the 13-dimensional action. In the \texttt{pd\_joint\_pos} controller of the ManiSkill Fetch agent, actions are ordered arm (7), gripper (1), body (3) and base (2), and the body group is head pan, head tilt and torso lift. Indices 8 and 9 are therefore the head, and the torso is index 10, which the client passes through unchanged. Because the RoboBenchMart robot class configures the body group for absolute position targets, writing zero centres the head. The initial torso height is 0.0 in the current environments, but that is the starting state, not a constraint the client imposes.

I do not think these properties explain the zeros on their own. The open-loop chunks and the chunk-boundary success check make success harder to register and might lower a rate, but upstream used the same chunk length and reports high rates on several of the same tasks. The predicate code, as far as the repository history shows, was not changed after the checkpoints were released, apart from a fix to how initial product poses are stored (\texttt{3de0ea7}, November 2025). I cannot fully rule out an interaction between the scene-wide disturbance check and the newer scenes, since the check uses a relative tolerance on world coordinates and fails an episode if any product in the store has moved; without trajectories I have no way of seeing whether that happened. They matter more for the future: once a version-aligned baseline exists, they are the next things to vary.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:results-summary}

The protocol ran to completion, with three small repairs, and left per-episode evidence rather than per-step trajectories. Under it, neither released checkpoint completed any of 1,800 pick-and-place episodes. $\pi_{0.5}$ closed doors in about a fifth of its attempts and almost never opened one; Octo closed a door four times in 180 attempts. Differences between Seeds, Pose and Layout are not resolvable at 30 episodes per configuration, door Layout does not change geometry, and Pairing had nothing to measure. The gap to the upstream report is far too large to be sampling noise. Version drift between the released checkpoints and the current environment is the leading candidate explanation, but it remains a hypothesis. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} turns these results into conclusions and sets out the experiments that would test it.

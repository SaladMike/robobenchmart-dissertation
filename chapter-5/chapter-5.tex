\section{Reading the results}

The empirical findings of this dissertation deliver a clear, unvarnished reality check. Inspect the primary evaluation results in \ref{tab:main-results}, and the initial impression is dominated by zeros: across all 1,620 evaluated pick-and-place rollouts, neither model completed a single successful item transfer. A superficial reading might dismiss these results as a failed evaluation. That reaction misses the core scientific value of benchmark research. These numbers represent an auditable, reproducible accounting of how released generalist vision--language--action checkpoints perform when confronted with an unforgiving rigid-body retail simulation.

To interpret this evidence with scientific rigor, the findings must be separated into three distinct evidentiary domains:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{System Usability and Simulation Feasibility}: Verifies whether the procedural store plans, mesh decimation pipelines, and ManiSkill3 physics environments step stably at scale.
  \item \textbf{Inference Interface and Execution Protocols}: Analyzes how heterogeneous VLA architectures (Octo vs. $\pi_{0.5}$) interface with a common multi-camera observation schema, and documents the operational characteristics of their inference servers.
  \item \textbf{Empirical Policy Competence}: Evaluates the physical performance of the released checkpoints across our 42-configuration evaluation ladder under progressive environmental shifts.
\end{enumerate}

Only the third domain reflects policy motor competence, and even there, performance is deeply shaped by low-level control interfaces, action chunk horizons, and kinematic constraints. Disentangling these mechanisms is the central objective of this chapter.

We deliberately avoid collapsing these results into a single scalar ranking. Averaging door closing scores against pick-and-place scores creates an uninterpretable metric: closing an unlatched freezer door and extracting a small box from a crowded shelf board involve fundamentally different contact dynamics, visual tolerances, and kinematics. Meaning emerges from reading across specific task dimensions and environmental tiers.

\section{RQ1: scene generation and computational usability}

Research Question 1 asked whether procedural store generation could synthesize structurally coherent, navigable store layouts without collapsing simulation throughput. On this operational criterion, the simulation pipeline succeeds. The procedural generator creates diverse floor layouts across random seeds rather than repeatedly shuffling furniture within a static room. Each layout serializes to a clean JSON descriptor, instantiates in ManiSkill3, and preserves continuous 2.0-meter aisle corridors. Fixtures coalesce into natural longitudinal multi-unit runs rather than scattering chaotically across the floor (\ref{fig:store-generation}).

The orientation tensor field acts as an effective directional filter along Cartesian sweeps. Because the decay parameter is configured to $d = 12$, its effective sensing radius collapses to roughly thirty centimeters, meaning candidate fixtures align almost exclusively with the single nearest boundary contour. Shelves snap strictly to coordinate axes. The resulting regularity is functional and robust, providing structured retail aisles while eliminating collision bottlenecks.

Shelf population achieves dense visual realism while holding occupancy constant. The experimental environments retain fully stocked shelves across all evaluations, controlling visual density so that policy variations reflect geometry and appearance rather than fluctuating stock gaps.

The decisive engineering test for RQ1 is plotted in \ref{fig:mesh-performance}, reported by RoboBenchMart~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart} and verified during our local deployment. When populating shelves with raw, unoptimized retail meshes, simulation stepping times surge from 0.21~seconds for a single unit to 1.24~seconds for four units, beyond which memory bottlenecks halt execution. In sharp contrast, Pareto-optimized simplified meshes maintain stepping durations between 0.23 and 0.42~seconds from one unit up to a massive 118-unit supermarket scene on a single GPU.

Crucially, \ref{fig:mesh-performance} illustrates an important design trade-off: below two units, raw meshes step marginally faster than simplified meshes due to the minor overhead of processing remeshed vertex buffers. As scene complexity scales, however, raw meshes overwhelm rendering memory while simplified proxies scale with remarkable flatness. Automated mesh decimation is the essential technical enabler that makes whole-store simulation practical on standard hardware.

Geometric accessibility holds by construction, as colliding proposals are culled during sweeping. Navigability is verified in terms of clearance envelopes: because evaluation episodes initialize 1.4~meters in front of target fixtures, store-scale navigation is not exercised by the current manipulation policies.

\section{RQ2: cross-paradigm inference and execution protocols}

Research Question 2 addressed the feasibility of serving heterogeneous VLA architectures through a unified observation--action interface, and investigated the operational characteristics of their execution.

The evaluation client successfully interfaces with both released policy checkpoints. A standardized schema---synchronizing three RGB camera streams (dual shoulder and wrist), proprioception, and a 13-dimensional continuous action vector---serves both the diffusion-based Octo and the flow-matching $\pi_{0.5}$ without architectural friction. Both models process language instructions and current sensory observations to predict continuous 50-step action chunks.

However, our execution audit revealed critical differences in server deployment and runtime concurrency:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo Runtime}: In the executed batch (\texttt{octo\_parallel\_20260928\_021558}), the first 17 configurations were evaluated serially, while the subsequent 25 configurations were distributed across four independent parallel Octo server instances. The server operated with an observation history window of one frame ($H_{\text{obs}} = 1$).
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$ Runtime}: In the executed batch (\texttt{pi05\_parallel\_20261002\_105333}), four simulation clients ran concurrently, all issuing remote procedure calls to a single shared OpenPI server process on port 8000.
\end{itemize}

Because asynchronous request queuing and pseudorandom number consumption sequences differ between these two execution frameworks, the two runs cannot be treated as a strictly paired, episode-by-episode comparison. Instead, they represent standardized benchmark assessments of each model's independent operational capability under the 42-configuration protocol.

As an inference pipeline, the framework fulfilled its mandate: all 42 configurations completed with exit code 0 across both models, generating 2,520 fully verified rollout logs and corresponding MP4 video recordings.

\section{RQ3: atomic-task performance and the task-type dichotomy}
\label{sec:atomic}

The core empirical contribution of this dissertation lies in answering RQ3. Across 2,520 evaluated rollouts, the data reveals a profound, structural performance dichotomy between task families.

\textbf{1. The Cluttered Pick-and-Place Collapse.} Across all pick-and-place tasks---Basket (picking into a cart), Floor (retrieving dropped goods), and Board (transferring between shelf tiers)---both Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$ score exactly zero (0/1,620 combined). Not a single product was successfully grasped, transported, and released across any of the four distribution-shift tiers. This complete collapse is not an artifact of a single broken task; it persists across three distinct fixture types, six retail products, and varying initial poses.

\textbf{2. Non-Zero Competence on Articulated Fixtures.} In stark contrast to pick-and-place, door manipulation reveals measurable, repeatable physical competence, particularly for $\pi_{0.5}$. Under the nominal Seeds tier, $\pi_{0.5}$ achieves a 25.0\% success rate on door closing (15/60). Under Pose perturbations, it maintains 16.7\% (10/60) on Close and 1.7\% (1/60) on Open. Under novel store Layouts, it achieves 20.0\% (12/60) on Close and 1.7\% (1/60) on Open. Octo registers isolated successes on showcase closing under Seeds (1/60, 1.7\%) and Layout (3/60, 5.0\%), totaling 4 successes overall.

\textbf{Physical Mechanics of the Performance Split.} Why does $\pi_{0.5}$ succeed on refrigerator and showcase doors while failing completely on shelf items? The answer lies in the fundamental contact mechanics of each task:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Door Manipulation}: Refrigerator and showcase doors feature broad, rigid surfaces constrained to a single rotational degree of freedom. When the Fetch end-effector approaches a door, the wide door face provides passive mechanical guidance. Pushing or dragging the door along its swing arc does not require sub-centimeter fingertip precision; the physical hinge constraint channels continuous contact force into valid rotational displacement, allowing the policy to succeed even with noticeable trajectory drift.
  \item \textbf{Cluttered Pick-and-Place}: Grasping a small consumer package (e.g., a Fanta can or Nivea box) wedged between neighbouring cartons requires extreme, closed-loop spatial precision. The parallel-jaw gripper must align its fingers within millimeter tolerances, approach along an unoccluded axis, and close with appropriate frictional hold. A positional error of merely two centimeters results in an outright collision with shelf boards or knocks neighbouring goods off the shelf, immediately violating our collateral disturbance predicate.
\end{itemize}

This comparison exposes an essential reality of current VLA checkpoints: they possess sufficient spatial awareness to engage large, physically constrained fixtures, but lack the fine-grained sensorimotor control required for contact-rich grasping in dense visual clutter.

\section{Distribution shift across the evaluation ladder}
\label{sec:results-shift}

Analyzing performance across the four evaluation tiers provides nuanced insights into policy robustness:

\textbf{Seeds to Pose (Local Chassis Perturbations).} The transition from Seeds to Pose holds all store geometry, lighting, and product identities constant, perturbing only the robot's initial chassis placement ($\pm 25$~cm translation, $\pm 22.5^{\circ}$ yaw). For $\pi_{0.5}$, door closing success drops from 25.0\% (15/60) under Seeds to 16.7\% (10/60) under Pose. This 8.3 percentage-point degradation confirms that even minor chassis offsets disrupt manipulation trajectories. The policy has partially memorized fixed open-loop trajectories relative to the nominal starting coordinate, struggling to compensate dynamically for base displacement.

\textbf{Pose to Layout (Visual and Geometric Shift).} The Layout condition introduces novel store floor plans, materials, and lighting perspectives. On door closing, $\pi_{0.5}$ records 20.0\% (12/60), slightly higher than its Pose score (16.7\%). Octo records 5.0\% (3/60) on Layout Close compared to 0.0\% on Pose. It is tempting to interpret this maintenance of performance as proof of robust geometric generalization. 

However, our protocol audit (Section~\ref{sec:verification}) uncovered a vital caveat: for door tasks, the nominal Layout condition does not instantiate disjoint store plans; it reuses the training floor layouts, varying only surface textures and initial base poses. Consequently, the door Layout condition tests texture and lighting invariance rather than geometric floor-plan adaptability. The fact that $\pi_{0.5}$ maintains a 20.0\% closing rate under novel textures demonstrates commendable visual robustness to surface finishes, but cannot be claimed as geometric transfer.

\textbf{Pairing (Compositional Generalization).} The Pairing condition evaluates zero-shot task--item binding by pairing familiar skills with products withheld from that task during training. Across all six applicable configurations (Basket, Floor, Board; 60 episodes each), both models score zero (0/180 per model). Because pick-and-place performance already sits at absolute zero under nominal Seeds and Pose conditions, the floor effect completely masks the compositional shift. We cannot determine whether the policy failed due to semantic grounding or low-level motor execution; the motor execution bottleneck prevents the hypothesis from being tested.

\section{Physical failure mechanisms}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

Detailed inspection of the 2,520 archived video recordings and execution logs reveals two severe physical constraints that explain the universal failure on pick-and-place tasks:

\textbf{1. The 50-Step Open-Loop Chunking Blind Spot.} Both models operate under an action chunk horizon of 50 steps. In ManiSkill3, the policy receives a sensory observation, generates 50 continuous low-level action commands, and the simulator executes all 50 steps open-loop before a new visual observation is rendered. In a cluttered physical environment, 50 simulation steps represent an immense blind spot. When the gripper fingers enter shelf clutter, the slightest unexpected contact or frictional slip deflects the gripper; unable to perceive this error mid-chunk, the policy continues executing its pre-planned trajectory, knocking adjacent products over or wedging against the shelf board. Open-loop chunking blinds the robot during the critical moment of grasp acquisition.

\textbf{2. The Rigid Spine Constraint ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$).} In the evaluation client, low-level logic explicitly overwrites the Fetch robot's torso-lift and head-pan action elements with zero at every timestep. Because the low-level controller interprets joint setpoints as absolute coordinates rather than incremental displacements, this zero-clamping forces the prismatic torso joint to remain permanently pinned at its mechanical minimum ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$). Across every rollout, the robot operates with a rigid, immobilized spine. 

This constraint severely cripples vertical workspace reach. For Floor recovery, reaching a fallen can on the ground and lifting it to a shelf board with an immobilized torso forces the 7-DOF arm to operate at the absolute boundary of its kinematic reach envelope, drastically reducing manipulability and magnifying joint velocity limits. For Board transfer, reaching upper shelf tiers becomes geometrically awkward. The robot is effectively forced to manipulate cluttered shelves with physical handcuffs.

The audit reveals that implementation discrepancies divide into two operational categories:

\section{What the benchmark demonstrates}

Synthesizing our empirical results and protocol audit establishes what this benchmark legitimately proves:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Verification of Released Checkpoints}: Publicly released fine-tuned checkpoints for Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$ possess measurable physical competence on articulated fixtures (closing doors up to 25.0\%), but remain completely incapable of reliable pick-and-place manipulation in dense retail clutter (0/1,620).
  \item \textbf{The Primacy of Contact Guidance}: Manipulation success in simulation is heavily determined by passive mechanical constraints. Fixtures with large surfaces and continuous rotational constraints succeed because they forgive trajectory drift, whereas unconstrained picking into clutter fails catastrophically under open-loop control.
  \item \textbf{The Necessity of Closed-Loop Control}: Evaluating multi-billion-parameter foundation models under a 50-step open-loop execution horizon evaluates open-loop trajectory luck rather than reactive intelligence. Closed-loop visual and tactile feedback is a mandatory prerequisite for robust manipulation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications and a targeted ablation programme}
\label{sec:ablations}

Rather than adding more models to an uninformative leaderboard of near-zero scores, our findings establish a concrete, prioritized diagnostic roadmap:

\textbf{1. Action Horizon Sweeps (Zero-Retraining Intervention).} The most urgent immediate experiment is evaluating existing checkpoints across varying action chunk horizons: $H \in \{1, 5, 10, 25, 50\}$. If grasp success emerges under tighter closed-loop replanning, the open-loop blindness hypothesis is directly confirmed without retraining a single model parameter.

\textbf{2. Unclamping Torso Kinematics.} Enabling the full 13-dimensional action vector by activating the prismatic torso joint ($q_{\text{torso}} > 0$) is essential to provide the vertical reach necessary for floor recovery and high-shelf picking.

\textbf{3. Truly Disjoint Door Layouts.} Door evaluation scenes should be regenerated using genuinely disjoint floor plan seeds, bringing door evaluation into methodological parity with the pick-and-place families.

\textbf{4. Enriching Demonstrations with Recovery Data.} Algorithmic demonstration collectors must incorporate intentional perturbations during pre-grasp and transport, recording corrective trajectories that teach imitation policies how to recover from inevitable controller drift.

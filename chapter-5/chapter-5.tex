\section{Reading the results}

Three kinds of evidence appear in this chapter and they answer different questions.

Scene and simulation measurements say whether the generated stores are usable at all---whether a plan can be built, instantiated and stepped quickly enough to run an experiment on. Demonstration and interface results say whether one adaptation pipeline can be laid across four architectures that share almost nothing internally. Policy success rates and failure labels say what happens when the scene changes underneath a trained policy. Only the third is about the models.

Keeping the three apart matters more than it sounds. A benchmark can be built correctly and still return nothing but low numbers, and those numbers are then a fact about the policies rather than a fault in the instrument. Much of this chapter is spent establishing which of the two a given result belongs to.

\ref{tab:main-results} carries the policy comparison. Values are mean success percentages over the applicable task--item--fixture combinations, with a hundred trials per triplet. A dash marks a condition the door tasks do not have; it is not a zero. The two composite columns are zero for every model and every scenario.

One thing the table deliberately does not do is collapse. \section{RQ1: scene generation and computational usability}

RQ1 asked for variation without loss of structure, and on the operational half of that question the pipeline delivers. Different seeds give different store plans rather than reshuffles of one room. Each plan serialises to JSON under a configuration hash, instantiates in ManiSkill3 and keeps its aisles open. Fixtures come out in coherent runs---rows of three to five units placed end to end, with free space between them---rather than scattered across the floor, as \ref{fig:store-generation} shows.

That structure deserves a careful sentence, because the mechanism producing it is coarser than the equations in the system chapter on their own suggest. Every shelf is axis-aligned. What the field decides is where one may sit, not which way it points, and with a decay coefficient of 12 that decision falls to whichever wall or fixture contour happens to lie within about thirty centimetres. The rows are real. They come from a gate applied along a sweep, not from continuous alignment to a smoothly blended orientation.

One sentence from the earlier draft of this section has to go entirely. Product placement was described as contributing controlled depletion alongside visual density. It contributes density. Depletion has no counterpart in the implementation and generated no scene in this study---every shelf, in every training and evaluation episode, is fully packed---and the figure that illustrated it has been removed from the system chapter along with the claim. What the generator was designed to do and what the study exercised were being reported as one thing, and they are not the same thing.

Computation is where RQ1 comes out cleanly, and \ref{fig:mesh-performance} says something sharper than a speed-up factor. Fifty simulation steps were timed on the stated Intel Xeon Gold 6278C and NVIDIA V100 as the number of stocked shelving units grew, comparing original product meshes against a setting where only the nearest unit keeps full detail.

The simplified series is almost flat. Scenes with one, eight and twenty units all cost about nine tenths of a second, and 118 units cost 1.20. The original series is not flat, and it does not go far: 0.60~seconds at one unit, 0.80 at two and 4.49 at four, where it stops. There are no measurements beyond four because at full mesh resolution the scene had already become impractical to step.

That is the finding, and it is a harder one than a ratio. Without simplification, this benchmark tops out at roughly four shelving units, which is a corner of a shop rather than a shop. With it, a 118-unit store runs faster than the four-unit original did. Asset geometry, not the physics solver, decides which experiments are possible here.

There are two honest qualifications. The headline contrast in earlier drafts---more than three times faster---pairs the 118-unit optimised scene against the four-unit original, so it moves scene size and mesh detail together and belongs to that pair of settings rather than to the optimisation alone. And the optimisation is not free at small scale: at one shelving unit, the simplified assets are the slower option, 0.90 seconds against 0.60, with the crossover somewhere between two and four units. Since the two series share only their smallest x-values, the figure contains no matched comparison in the range where simplification actually matters. Producing one means running the original meshes at eight units and above, which is precisely what the missing orange points suggest was not feasible.

A separate throughput measurement varied the number of parallel environments, stepping random actions through a navigation environment and recording aggregate frame rate and device memory. It is worth being clear about what that measures. No policy, no task, no success---simulation and rendering cost and nothing else.

The two observation modes behave differently in kind rather than in degree, and an earlier draft of this section obscured that by reporting diminishing returns in both. Only the rendered mode diminishes. With state observations, aggregate throughput rises close to linearly out to 128 environments while device memory grows by a couple of gigabytes. With RGB observations, it flattens well before sixty-four environments, and memory grows several times faster, because every additional environment adds geometry traversal, shading and an image transfer on top of its physics step. Matched at the same number of environments, rendering costs roughly an order of magnitude in throughput and several times the memory.

Two regimes follow: state-only scenes when the bottleneck is sample count and rendered scenes when the image is the point, as it is for every policy result in this dissertation. In that second regime, a sixteen-gigabyte card is the binding constraint well before the simulator is.

The underlying numbers are not reproduced here. They were recorded during the study but the result files were not retained alongside the figure, and reporting digitised values would give them a precision they no longer have. The qualitative contrast is robust to that; the exact frame rates are not, and anyone planning capacity should rerun the sweep on their own hardware, which the measurement procedure supports directly.

So RQ1 is answered yes on generation and yes on cost. On the rest of its own wording, it is answered less comfortably, and the wording is worth going back to: scenes that are collision-free, navigable and visually plausible.

Collision-free holds by construction, since a candidate that fails the footprint or passage test is never placed. Visually plausible was never measured. No realism study, no comparison against a distribution of surveyed store plans, no physical validation---the scenes look like stores to me, and that is the whole of the evidence. Navigable holds geometrically and was never exercised: the plans leave two metres of passage and support base motion, and not one episode in this study required a robot to use it, because every episode begins 1.4~metres from the fixture it needs. Section~\ref{sec:verification} takes that up properly.

A generator can therefore be judged sound on the criteria it was built against while leaving the most interesting of those criteria untested. That is the position here.

\section{RQ2: demonstration generation and adaptation}

The collection covers twelve task--item--fixture triplets: 248 successful episodes apiece, 2,976 trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions. It took about eight hours of wall-clock time on a single V100. Anchor decomposition with screw motion and an RRT-Connect fallback reached that without anyone touching a teleoperation rig, and one observation--action schema then fed four model families whose internals have almost nothing in common. As a supervision source, the pipeline does what it was built to do.

Two things about the corpus are thinner than the totals imply. The layout pool behind it is smaller than the documentation states, because two configuration files meant to generate different stores for board transfer were given the same seed, as were the two for floor recovery; those tasks draw on five distinct plans rather than ten. And the fallback structure is less redundant than the methodology chapter describes---the second screw attempt repeats the first call with identical arguments, which for a deterministic solver cannot change anything. Neither issue costs a result. Both narrow what ``scalable and diverse'' is entitled to mean here.

The more revealing comparison sits in the appendix rather than the main table, and it is the one between Seeds and Pose. Nothing about the scene changes between those two columns. The store is the same store; the shelf holds the same products in the same places; and the instruction is word for word what it was during collection. All that moves is where the robot happens to be standing when the episode starts. The bounds differ by task and neither is large: roughly twenty centimetres of retreat and a quarter of a metre sideways for basket picking, a little under half a metre on both axes for board transfer, with about twenty-two degrees of yaw in either case.

Octo goes from 58\%, 35\% and 30\% on its three board-transfer products to 30\%, 16\% and 17\%. $\pi_{0.5}$ goes from 82\%, 89\% and 82\% on pick-to-basket to 48\%, 60\% and 58\%.

Both roughly halve, and they halve before a single texture or wall has moved. Whatever these policies learned from 248 demonstrations per combination, a good part of it was tied to standing in the right place. That is a stronger statement about brittleness than any of the unseen-scene numbers, because it needs no argument about what counts as a distribution shift.

SmolVLA is a harder case and a more uncomfortable one. It records zero on every reported pick-and-place product, in every condition, including the training seeds---while still opening and closing the refrigerated fixture at rates between nineteen and thirty-one percent. A model that never once places a product in the basket it was shown 248 times is not obviously reporting on the data.

I cannot say what happened. Capacity, pretraining mixture, action normalisation and optimisation schedule are all candidates, and this experiment was built to compare policies under shift rather than to diagnose one of them, so any single attribution would be invention. What can be said is narrower: a corpus sufficient for three model families to reach double-digit success was not sufficient for the fourth, and that asymmetry belongs to the pair of them rather than to either alone. A zero row is also the point at which a benchmark stops being informative about a model, which is worth remembering before reading anything into SmolVLA's later columns.

RQ2 is therefore answered yes, with the qualification doing real work. The pipeline produces a reproducible corpus, and that corpus is sufficient to adapt heterogeneous architectures and elicit retail behaviour that is not zero. It is not sufficient to produce dependable behaviour in any of them, and for one of them it produced none at all.

Part of the reason is visible in what the collector keeps. It keeps successes, and only successes. The policy learns what a nominal planned trajectory looks like; it receives less evidence about how to correct a base error or reattempt a missed grasp. The prevalence of pre-grasp and grasp failures is consistent with this limitation, although the present experiments do not prove a causal link.

They also do not rule out a competing account, which Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes} returns to. The evaluation client requests a fifty-step action chunk and executes all of it before constructing the next observation, so a policy sees the scene between ten and twenty times across a whole episode and spends the intervening steps committed to a plan it cannot revise. Missing recovery data and missing opportunity to recover produce the same failure label.

\section{RQ3: atomic-task performance}
\label{sec:atomic}

No model is good at this. That is the first thing the Pose column says, and it is worth saying before any comparison starts.

$\pi_{0.5}$ comes closest. It clears 55\% on pick-to-basket, 56\% on board transfer, 56\% on opening the fridge and 91\% on closing it, then drops to 21\% on floor recovery---a task that looks easier than any of them. $\pi_0$ is close to the mirror image: 61\% and 95\% on the two door tasks, better than $\pi_{0.5}$ manages on either, and between 14\% and 21\% across all three pick-and-place families. Octo sits lower throughout, at 21\% on basket picking and board transfer, 27\% and 45\% on the doors and 1\% on the floor. SmolVLA is non-zero in exactly two cells of the main table. Both of them are doors.

So there is no ranking, only rankings. Ask which model is strongest and the honest response is another question: at what?

Doors are the easiest column for three of the four, and the reason is contact tolerance rather than anything cognitive. A door is large; its hinge constrains the motion to a single degree of freedom; and a shove landing almost anywhere along the panel will close it. A packet on a shelf offers none of that forgiveness. The gripper has to arrive at a particular pose, from a particular direction, without touching what sits beside it.

Opening runs consistently below closing for both $\pi$ models---95 against 61 for $\pi_0$, 91 against 56 for $\pi_{0.5}$---and the gap has a plainly mechanical source. Closing needs contact. Opening needs a handle: acquired, held and kept held through the swing. That is a grasp problem wearing an articulation problem's clothes, and Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes} shows it failing as one.

This leaves floor recovery, the result that does not fit.

Nothing crowds the object. It lies in the open, unobstructed, with no neighbouring stock to disturb; by every intuition about clutter, it should be the easiest pick in the suite. It is the hardest. Octo manages 1\%; $\pi_{0.5}$ manages 21\%; and the two strongest models are separated by a factor of twenty on a task neither of them finds simple.

Part of that is whole-body coordination. The robot has to bring the arm down to floor level, hold the base steady while it does, then lift the product back to a shelf group above waist height. Part of it is a constraint that only surfaced during the verification described in Section~\ref{sec:verification}: the torso does not move. It starts at its lowest position and stays there for the whole episode, in every task, so the vertical distance between the floor and the destination board has to be covered by the arm alone. Floor recovery is the one task in the suite where that distance approaches what the arm can reach.

The lesson survives the caveat and is arguably sharper for it. Difficulty here is not a function of visual clutter. It is a function of what the body has to do, and a task can be visually trivial while remaining mechanically close to infeasible.

\section{Distribution shift}
\label{sec:results-shift}

A new store reduces performance for almost every non-trivial model--task pair. For $\pi_{0.5}$, the absolute loss from Pose to Layout is 24 percentage points on basket picking, 17 on floor recovery, 29 on board transfer, 7 on opening and 13 on closing. For $\pi_0$, the losses are 13, 15, 16, 17 and 10 points. Octo loses 19 points on basket picking, none on its already low floor rate, 20 on board transfer, 4 on opening and 19 on closing.

The loss is not proportional to the starting score. $\pi_{0.5}$'s board transfer collapses from 56\% to 27\%; its door closing holds 78\% against 91\% under Pose. Read quickly, that looks like a finding about articulation---large rigid geometry survives visual change, small packages do not.

I no longer think the comparison supports it.

Preparing this chapter, I went back through the evaluation scripts and found that the two task families had not been shifted equally. Pick-and-place was evaluated on scene directories built from a disjoint seed family, so its unseen condition really did change store layout, aisle structure and surface appearance together. The door tasks were not. Their unseen runs point at the same generated scene directory used to collect their training demonstrations; only the episode seed and the initial-pose seed differ, which varies textures and starting pose and nothing else. The fridge configuration generates a single scene. A single layout is used for both training and testing.

So the defensible statement is narrower and duller than the one I set out to make. Under a weaker shift, door tasks lose less. Whether they would still lose less under a matched shift has not been tested. The mechanism suggested above---larger and more consistent contact geometry, an instruction that never names a product---is not refuted by this; it simply has no evidence behind it yet. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} specifies the experiment that would supply some, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} sets out how the discrepancy came to light.

SmolVLA's opening score rises from 10\% to 13\% under Layout, and its closing value stays at 13\%. This should not be described as beneficial generalisation. At low success rates, finite trials and item aggregation can produce small reversals. Without confidence intervals, the three-point change is not enough to support a claim of improvement.

Pairing is the sharpest reported test. Octo becomes zero on all three applicable tasks. $\pi_0$ retains only 1\% on floor recovery. $\pi_{0.5}$ records 5\% on pick-to-basket, 3\% on pick-from-floor and 21\% on board transfer. The detailed $\pi_{0.5}$ table shows substantial item variation: board transfer reaches 34\% for Nivea and 7\% for Fanta; floor recovery reaches 3\% for Duff and 2\% for Fanta; basket picking reaches 7\% for Nestle and 2\% for SLAM. The task-level means therefore conceal both successful transfer pockets and near-complete failures.

These results answer RQ3a directly. Adapted policies are sensitive to relatively modest retail-specific shifts. Known products do not automatically transfer to a task in which they were not demonstrated, and a policy that succeeds in a familiar store can lose a large fraction of that performance under new layout and texture. $\pi_{0.5}$ provides the strongest evidence of partial transfer, not reliable transfer.

One attribution has to be withdrawn before those sentences are safe to quote. It is tempting to explain the Layout loss partly by changed navigation geometry, since the layout moves and the aisles move with it. Episode initialisation does not permit that reading. The robot is placed 1.4~metres in front of the correct fixture and turned to face it at the start of every episode, in every condition, with randomisation on the order of a quarter of a metre and twenty-two degrees applied on top. Nothing in the reported protocol asks a policy to cross a store or to choose between distant fixtures. What changes between Pose and Layout is the visual context and the final metre of approach, and the twenty-nine-point drop on board transfer has to be accounted for inside that smaller space.

\section{Composite tasks}

Every composite-task entry is zero. This is the clearest result in the table and the easiest to overstate. The evaluation does not show that a model cannot understand a multi-step natural-language request, because an oracle supplies atomic instructions. It shows that none of the tested adapted policies completed the full execution chain under the protocol.

Compounding atomic error provides a strong baseline explanation. If three stages each succeeded with probability 0.5 and were independent, complete success would be 12.5\%. With a 0.2 atomic rate, it would be 0.8\%. The actual stages are dependent: a poor placement may alter camera views or block the next approach, and the robot state after one completion differs from a clean reset. Zero observed success is therefore compatible with the atomic rates without requiring a separate language-planning failure.

The finding still matters operationally. Retail value comes from finished orders, not isolated benchmark primitives. A policy that occasionally picks one item but cannot sustain a short sequence is not ready for unattended fulfilment.

A column of zeros is also a poor way to report it, and the reporting is the part that was avoidable. Each composite environment already returns a per-subtask completion flag and a normalised count of how many subtasks the episode reached, alongside the binary success value that became the column above. That information distinguishes a policy failing on its first pick from one failing at the final release. It was computed during evaluation and dropped at the aggregation stage, so recovering it costs no new simulation, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} treats the omission as an error in this dissertation rather than a gap in the benchmark.

\section{Failure modes}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

Target grounding is the second-largest category for three models and remains 20\% even for $\pi_{0.5}$. The benchmark therefore exposes a coupled perception--action problem. A policy can generate plausible movement while acting on the wrong branded product. Since the success predicate checks the named item, visually convincing but semantically incorrect manipulation is rejected.

Movement and pre-grasp labels distinguish two spatial scales. SmolVLA's 16\% movement share and 32\% pre-grasp share indicate breakdown before contact. Octo records 8\% and 9\%, while $\pi_{0.5}$ records only 1\% and 2\%. The larger model more often reaches interaction, but reaching the object does not solve the task: half of its labelled failures occur at grasp.

Post-grasp side effects expose a trade. $\pi_{0.5}$ has a 7\% displacement share, higher than the other policies. A model that reaches and interacts more often also creates more opportunities to disturb neighbouring items. Strict side-effect checking is therefore important; otherwise an aggressive shelf sweep could appear as progress.

Scenario-level plots show failures shifting earlier as distribution shift increases. In familiar conditions, Octo and the $\pi$ models often reach the object and fail at grasp. Under Pairing, grounding and approach failures occupy a larger share for several models. $\pi_{0.5}$ retains a comparatively consistent path to contact, but its grasp remains unreliable.

One movement in \ref{fig:failure-distribution} is worth separating out, because averaging over scenarios in \ref{tab:failure-share} conceals it. The \emph{task} label---the policy pursuing an operation the instruction did not ask for---is close to absent for Octo on training seeds, in-domain and unseen scenes. Under Pairing it accounts for roughly half of its failures. When the \emph{target} share is added, close to nine in ten Octo failures in that condition are cases of doing the wrong thing or acting on the wrong object, with almost nothing left over for contact. SmolVLA moves the same way, to about a third \emph{task}. The $\pi$ models do not: \emph{task} stays near zero for both.

That is a different kind of degradation from the one the aggregate table suggests. It is not a policy that understands the request and executes it imprecisely. It is a policy that, once the scene and the target pairing are both unfamiliar, stops tracking the instruction and falls back on some default behaviour---frequently the operation it saw most often during adaptation. Section~\ref{sec:verification} is relevant here too, since the products in this condition were visible throughout training and the collapse is therefore not a failure to recognise anything. This pattern explains why better high-level grounding alone will not close the benchmark: contact precision is an independent bottleneck.

Before that bottleneck is attributed to the policies, two properties of the evaluation setup deserve to sit next to it, because both bear directly on grasping and neither was visible from the results alone.

The first is the execution horizon. A policy is queried once and returns fifty actions; those fifty actions are executed before it observes anything again. Half of $\pi_{0.5}$'s labelled failures occur at the moment of grasp, after it has already reached the correct product---and a wrist that closes two centimetres off target cannot be corrected until the chunk has run out, whatever the model has learned about correcting it. The demonstration corpus and the execution horizon are two different explanations for the same label, and the experiment reported here does not separate them.

The second is the torso. It does not move. Episode initialisation places the prismatic joint at its lowest position and the evaluation client zeroes the corresponding action element before every step, so vertical reach is fixed for the whole episode in every task. That is worth holding against the floor-recovery result in particular: retrieving an object from the ground and returning it to a shelf above waist height, with a body that cannot rise, is a harder problem than Section~\ref{sec:atomic} describes. Section~\ref{sec:verification} works through both properties and what they cost.

Splitting the same labels by task family, in \ref{fig:failure-by-tasktype}, sharpens two of these readings. Door failures for $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ sit almost entirely in \emph{grasp} and \emph{partial}: the handle is reached and then either not secured or swung too little. SmolVLA's door failures sit in \emph{move} and \emph{pregrasp} instead, which is a different task from the one the other three are attempting. And \emph{place-coord} appears almost exclusively in floor recovery, where the destination is a position on a shelf rather than a basket riding on the robot---the one task in the suite whose goal must be located in the world.

The direction for the next method follows from the shape of all this rather than from any single number. It calls for stronger item-level grounding, contact-aware action adaptation and demonstrations that show recovery. Scaling language capacity alone leaves an unstable gripper pose exactly where it was.

\section{Independent verification of the experimental protocol}
\label{sec:verification}

While the results above were being produced, I asked a fellow student to read my experimental implementation against the protocol as it is written in the system and methodology chapters and report any apparent disagreements. This is the one check an author cannot perform reliably alone: I knew what the system was supposed to do, and that knowledge is exactly what makes it hard to see what it actually does.

The review came back with a list of possible discrepancies. I reproduced each one myself against the code and the stored configurations before accepting it. Everything reported in this section is something I confirmed; anything I could not confirm is marked as such below rather than presented as fact.

A second group of items changes no number in this chapter but does change what the system can be said to do. The tensor field admits or rejects axis-aligned placements rather than orienting shelves continuously. The action vector carries thirteen elements rather than eleven. Stock depletion and vertical stacking were designed but were not used to generate any scene in this study. These are corrected in the system chapter and listed here only so that the correction is visible from the results.

One item resisted confirmation and is reported as unresolved. The evaluation server for Octo runs with a history length of one, while the training configuration in Appendix~\ref{app:training-results} records two history observations. I was not able to establish which setting produced the reported numbers, so Octo's figures should be read as a lower bound on what that checkpoint can do, and no conclusion in this dissertation rests on the difference.

Line the two directions up and the shape of the finding becomes clear. Almost everything that diverged on the perception and generalisation axes diverged toward leniency: the unseen products were visible throughout training, the unseen scenes for half the task suite were the training scenes, the robot began each episode aimed at the right fixture from close range, the success box was generous and the collateral check partial. Had the experiment run exactly as the system and methodology chapters describe, every one of those conditions would have been harder.

The scores would have gone down. They were already near zero across the strongest shift for three of four models and zero everywhere on both composite tasks.

A negative result obtained under conditions more forgiving than intended is a stronger negative result, not a weaker one, and the central claim of this chapter survives the verification with more room to spare than the original design would have given it. What does not survive is part of the diagnostic layer, and that is the real cost. Two of the more quotable interpretations offered above rested on comparisons the experiment did not actually run. Articulated fixtures may or may not be more robust to shift, since the door tasks were never subjected to the same shift. Grasp may dominate the failure distribution because planner-shaped demonstrations teach no recovery or because a policy blind for fifty steps has no opportunity to recover whatever it learned. Both now stand as hypotheses with experiments attached rather than as findings.

The verification also produced one straightforwardly useful correction. The composite evaluation already records a per-subtask completion flag and a normalised count of subtasks reached, alongside the binary success value that became the column of zeros in \ref{tab:main-results}. The information needed to distinguish a policy that fails on its first pick from one that reaches the final release was computed and then discarded at the reporting stage. Recovering it requires no new simulation.

\section{What the benchmark demonstrates}

The evidence supports three claims. First, scene variation that appears modest to a person---a new layout, texture or task--item association---can produce a large policy loss. Second, retail difficulty is not reducible to product recognition. Mobile approach, pre-grasp geometry, contact and placement all contribute. Third, generalist pretraining plus a few hundred demonstrations per combination yields partial adaptation but not dependable composite execution under this protocol.

The evidence does not identify a single winning model property. $\pi_{0.5}$ is strongest overall, yet $\pi_0$ leads on some door conditions. Parameter count, pretraining diversity, architecture and fine-tuning recipe change together. Nor does the comparison establish that retail-specific pretraining is necessary in a strict causal sense. It makes that direction plausible because task-specific fine-tuning leaves substantial failures, but a controlled pretraining ablation would be required to confirm it.

The practical conclusion is sharper than a leaderboard ranking. The most valuable improvements are likely to be measurable at intermediate stages: correct target selection, reachable base placement, pre-grasp error, grasp retention, collateral displacement and subtask depth. The proposed benchmark already exposes many of these concepts through annotation and state. Turning them into automatic, episode-level metrics would make the next generation of comparisons more diagnostic and statistically robust.

One qualification governs everything in this section. The protocol that produced these numbers is not in every respect the one the system and methodology chapters describe, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} sets out the confirmed differences. Several conditions turned out to be more forgiving than intended---unseen items that were visible throughout training, unseen scenes that were the training scenes for half the task suite, a robot that always began aimed at the right fixture---and a smaller number turned out to be harsher. The aggregate claim above survives that accounting; two of the more quotable interpretations do not.

\section{Implications and a targeted ablation programme}
\label{sec:ablations}

The results suggest a sequence of experiments more useful than immediately adding another model to the leaderboard.

Two of them come directly out of Section~\ref{sec:verification} and should be run before the rest, because until they are, parts of \ref{tab:main-results} cannot be interpreted at all. One is a matched shift for the door tasks: regenerate their evaluation scenes from a seed family disjoint from training, as the pick-and-place tasks already do, and re-measure the Pose-to-Layout loss. If articulated fixtures still lose less, the mechanism proposed in Section~\ref{sec:results-shift} has support. If the gap closes, the contrast was an artefact of the protocol. The other is a sweep over the execution horizon. Re-evaluating an unchanged checkpoint at horizons of one, five, ten and fifty costs no retraining, and if grasp failures fall as the horizon shortens then a substantial share of what the taxonomy currently attributes to contact precision belongs to open-loop execution instead.

The remaining ablations concern the policies rather than the instrument. The first should isolate vision from geometry. Policies could be evaluated on identical layouts with textures changed, identical textures with layouts changed and identical scenes with product pose changed. The present Layout condition combines texture and layout, so a 29-point board-transfer loss for $\pi_{0.5}$ cannot be assigned to appearance or navigation. Factorial evaluation would reveal whether augmentation should focus on image statistics, whole-body approach geometry or both.

A second ablation should vary the number and diversity of demonstrations. The configuration used here fixes 248 trajectories per triplet. Training subsets at several sizes would show whether performance is data-limited or whether additional planner-shaped trajectories saturate. Diversity can be changed independently by widening anchor perturbations, varying shelf occupancy and adding more layouts. If twice as many nearly identical successes produce little gain while a smaller diverse set helps, collection effort should shift from volume to coverage.

Recovery data is the next priority. A controlled dataset could perturb the base before pre-grasp, offset the wrist near contact, weaken a grasp or place the object at the edge of the target region. Demonstrations would then show corrective behaviour. Evaluation should measure recovery probability conditional on the perturbation rather than only final task success. This directly tests the hypothesis raised by the failure distribution: policies may know the nominal sequence yet lack a route back after small errors.

Grounding and control should also be separated. One experiment can provide an oracle target mask or 3D target pose while leaving action generation unchanged. If wrong-target errors disappear but grasp remains dominant, perception was only part of the problem. The complementary experiment can retain the policy's target selection but hand the selected object to a privileged grasp planner. The gap between these conditions would quantify the contribution of manipulation precision. Neither oracle represents deployment; both are diagnostic interventions.

Door tasks offer a useful calibration family. Their relatively high success allows controlled changes in handle shape, door mass, friction and required opening distance. Because the instruction remains simple, performance differences can be attributed more directly to articulation and contact. The main result suggests closing is easier than opening. Varying whether the gripper must acquire a handle or may push a surface would test that interpretation.

Composite tasks need richer outputs. Every episode should report the number of completed subtasks, the state at each hand-off and conditional success from stage $k$ to $k+1$. Reset-free continuation can then be compared with an artificial clean reset between atomic stages. If clean-reset composition succeeds while continuous composition does not, state drift and observation change are central. If both remain zero, atomic reliability or instruction switching is sufficient to explain the failure.

Retail-specific pretraining is a plausible response, but it needs a fair test. One model architecture should be pretrained on matched quantities of general manipulation data, synthetic retail data and a mixture. Fine-tuning and evaluation would remain fixed. Such a study could determine whether the retail domain contributes beyond dataset size. The current cross-model table cannot do this because architecture, scale and pretraining mixture all vary together.

Finally, success must be connected to safety and throughput. A policy that completes 60\% of picks but displaces stock in many failures may be less useful than a slower conservative policy. Future reporting could include time to completion, collision impulse, non-target displacement magnitude, recovery count and energy or path length. These are not substitutes for task success. They describe the cost of obtaining it.

This ablation programme follows directly from the benchmark evidence. It treats low scores as a map of unanswered questions, not a verdict that larger models will inevitably solve or fail at retail. The benchmark is most valuable when it narrows the next experiment.

\section{Retail manipulation as a test for robot policies}

A tin of Heinz beans has fallen off a shelf. It lies on the floor somewhere between 0.7 and 1.1~m in front of the shelving unit, and a Fetch robot stands about 1.4~m back, facing the shelf. The instruction reads \emph{``pick Heinz Beans in a rich tomato sauce from floor and place it on shelf''}. To succeed, the robot has to find the right tin, drive close enough for its arm to reach the floor, pick the tin up, and set it down within 20~cm of the spot it fell from. Then it has to stop moving. And if, along the way, it has nudged any other product in the store out of place, the attempt counts as a failure.

Nothing in that list is exotic. Recognising a label, positioning a base, grasping, placing without collateral damage: each is a well-studied problem. What a shop does is pile them into one aisle and make every one of them a little tighter than it would be on a laboratory table, with packaging that looks alike, shelves that leave little room, and doors on the refrigerated units that have to be swung through an arc while the base keeps out of the way.

That makes retail a demanding place to test vision-language-action (VLA) policies, models that turn camera images and a written instruction directly into robot actions. Several are now public, along with recipes for fine-tuning them on new robots. A separate question gets much less attention. When someone other than the original authors runs a released, fine-tuned checkpoint, does it do what was reported? Benchmark papers rarely check each other on this.

RoboBenchMart~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart} is a recent benchmark for exactly this setting. Built on ManiSkill3~\cite{taomaniskill3}, it generates dark-store layouts procedurally, stocks them with a library of retail products, and defines pick-and-place and door tasks for a Fetch mobile manipulator. Its authors generated motion-planned demonstrations, fine-tuned four VLA policies on them, evaluated the policies under four increasingly demanding conditions, and released the code, the scenes and some of the fine-tuned checkpoints. That release is the starting point of this dissertation.

\section{Problem statement}

A benchmark result is only as useful as it is reproducible. In robot learning the gap between ``the code is public'' and ``the result can be reproduced'' is wide. A result depends on the policy weights, on the version of the simulator and the environment code, on how the policy is served and how its actions are applied, and on details of the evaluation protocol that a paper may summarise in a sentence. A change in any of these can change the outcome, and the changes are not always visible from the outside.

This dissertation asks a narrow version of that question for RoboBenchMart. If I take two of the released checkpoints, Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$, and run them through the benchmark's atomic evaluation protocol using only what is publicly available today, what results do I get? How do they compare with the published ones? And what can and cannot be concluded from the comparison? I did not train any model. I did not re-run the upstream study. I ran the released artefacts as released, recorded what happened, and examined the protocol closely enough to say what the numbers mean.

\section{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.8em,style=nextline]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can the 42 atomic evaluation configurations of RoboBenchMart be run to completion using only the public code, scenes and checkpoints? What has to be repaired, and what evidence does the run leave behind?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How many episodes does each checkpoint complete in each of the five task families under each condition, and which differences between conditions and between models can be resolved with 30 episodes per configuration?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How far are these results from the upstream report, and how do properties of the protocol and differences between software versions limit the conclusions that can be drawn?
\end{description}

\section{Scope}

The study covers two policies, Octo~\cite{octo_2023} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld}, as fine-tuned and released by the RoboBenchMart authors. Each was evaluated on the 42 atomic configurations defined in the benchmark's evaluation script: 12 under each of the Seeds, Pose and Layout conditions and 6 under Pairing, with 30 episodes per configuration. That is 1,260 episodes per model.

The upstream benchmark covers more than this, so it helps to be explicit about what I left out. I evaluated neither $\pi_0$ nor SmolVLA, both of which appear in the upstream paper. I ran no composite tasks. I did not annotate why episodes failed, so the upstream failure taxonomy is described but not applied. I did not fine-tune or train anything, and the training conditions of the checkpoints are known to me only from the upstream paper. All experiments are in simulation.

One further boundary turned out to matter more than I expected. The code and scenes I evaluated on are newer than the checkpoints. The environment has changed in several ways since the checkpoints were published, and I did not attempt to reconstruct the version the checkpoints were trained against. The results therefore describe the released checkpoints on the current release of the benchmark, and Chapter~\ref{chap:results} discusses what that implies.

\section{Contributions}

What this dissertation adds is modest and practical. At its centre is an independent, fully documented run of the RoboBenchMart atomic protocol for two released checkpoints: 2,520 episodes, each tied to a seed, a log line, a video and a SHA-256 hash, with the revision of every component recorded alongside.

The run would not have happened without some repair work. The floor environments could not even be constructed, because an upstream commit had removed an import that was still in use. The evaluation client read its instruction from a wrapped environment and failed. The Octo server kept one observation history for all connections, so parallel clients would have mixed each other's episodes. I fixed all three and documented the fixes.

The results are a contribution in their own right, if not a cheerful one. Neither checkpoint completed any of 1,800 pick-and-place episodes, and $\pi_{0.5}$ closed doors in about a fifth of its attempts, numbers that sit far below the upstream figures by a margin sampling noise cannot cover.

Reading the evaluation code closely also let me correct several statements about the protocol. One of them was my own: an earlier draft said the client fixes the robot's torso, when it actually locks the head. Combing the upstream history, I identify the changes between the checkpoints and the evaluated environment most likely to matter, and propose a version-aligned re-evaluation as the way to test them.

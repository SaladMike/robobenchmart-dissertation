\section{Retail manipulation as an embodied stress test}

Moving a soup can between shelf boards seems almost trivial. For an embodied agent, it is a cascade of fragile assumptions. The visual prompt specifies a product whose label differs from its adjacent competitor by subtle typography; the target sits hemmed in by fragile cereal cartons; the parallel gripper must enter a three-centimetre gap without triggering a shelf avalanche; and a mobile base stopping just five centimetres shallow turns an arm motion from a secure grasp into an outright miss. Articulated fixtures exacerbate the strain. A freezer door requires approaching at a constrained angle, latching onto a slim handle, and coordinating mobile base repositioning through the arc of the swing. A multi-item customer order adds memory, sequencing, and tolerance for accumulated drift. Each obstacle is familiar in isolation. Their collision in a single physical space turns retail manipulation into an unforgiving stress test for embodied artificial intelligence.

Tabletop benchmarks have driven remarkable progress in robot imitation learning, yet they largely insulate policies from the physical friction of logistics. Sprawling image datasets confirm whether a detector can locate merchandise on a rack, but passive perception cannot tell whether a physical mobile manipulator can navigate an aisle, align its chassis, form a stable contact-rich grasp, and deposit the payload without collateral damage. Conversely, an isolated tabletop or single-shelf setup risks creating a false sense of security, exposing kinematic slip while masking the dramatic layout and lighting shifts encountered between store aisles. A meaningful retail benchmark must treat the physical store, the task semantics, the demonstration corpus, and the evaluation protocol as a tightly coupled system.

This dissertation explores that coupled challenge through an independent reproduction, adaptation, and empirical stress-testing of RoboBenchMart, an embodied retail manipulation benchmark implemented in ManiSkill3~\cite{taomaniskill3}. ManiSkill3 supplies GPU-parallel physics and multi-camera rendering, enabling repeatable evaluations at a scale impractical to reset manually in a physical brick-and-mortar shop. The system pairs procedural store-plan generation and textured asset curation with motion-planned expert demonstrations. Rather than proposing another speculative foundation architecture, this study focuses on an immediate engineering question: what happens when a practitioner takes publicly released, generalist vision-language-action (VLA) policies, fine-tunes them on a shared retail demonstration corpus using a single consumer graphics card, and systematically steps them through increasingly unfamiliar environments?

The compute budget was not an abstraction; it was an uncompromising physical boundary. Every adaptation pass was executed on a lone NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Training ran under fixed step counts without validation checkpoints, batch size was capped at eight, and the larger architectures relied on parameter-efficient low-rank adaptation rather than full fine-tuning. Unsurprisingly, none of the adapted policies achieved dependable task execution; task success under pose perturbations stalled between zero and four percent. Yet writing off near-zero performance as a simple failure misses the primary scientific value of benchmark engineering. The utility of this study lies in the diagnostic scaffolding erected around those numbers: an independent four-stage evaluation ladder separating pose, layout, and task--item pairing; an earliest-primary failure taxonomy classifying 2,906 unsuccessful episodes; and a forensic protocol audit comparing the written experimental design against runtime reality. The transfer question remains empirically unanswered, but where and why the manipulation chain collapses is made plain.

\section{Problem statement}

The central issue motivating this work is the scarcity of an end-to-end evaluation pipeline that simultaneously combines language-conditioned mobile manipulation, dense retail clutter, and disciplined distribution shifts under a unified observation--action schema. 

While existing benchmarks supply individual pieces of this puzzle---object datasets support recognition, physics engines supply contact mechanics, and robot datasets provide pretraining weights---they rarely force a policy through a controlled progression from a familiar scene to an altered layout, and finally to an unseen skill--object pairing. This void leaves two interpretive blind spots. First, claims of generalisation remain ambiguous: a policy might succeed merely because it memorised a static camera angle or background wallpaper, rather than acquiring an adaptable manipulation primitive. Second, aggregate failure rates are diagnostically opaque. When a policy scores zero, an undifferentiated failure provides no clue whether the model hallucinated the instruction, misgrounded the item, failed to steer the mobile base, clipped a neighbouring carton, or dropped the product during transport. A benchmark must make these breakdown modes separable.

The dissertation addresses these gaps through three guiding research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.8em,style=nextline]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How can retail layouts, fixtures, and shelf populations be procedurally generated with sufficient structural variation for generalisation studies while strictly preserving collision bounds and passage space?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Can a reproducible, anchor-based motion-planning pipeline generate demonstrations adequate for fine-tuning diverse generalist VLA policies on mobile retail tasks without human teleoperation?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do adapted policies behave as evaluation progresses from familiar training poses to unseen store layouts and novel task--item combinations, and at which manipulation stage do failures primarily occur?
\end{description}

The answers delivered here are nuanced and grounded in empirical reality. RQ1 is answered affirmatively through tensor-field orientation gating and multi-objective mesh simplification. RQ2 is answered affirmatively for data generation (2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions feeding four distinct architectures), but only weakly for policy adaptation, where training sufficiency was unverified. RQ3 yields no evidence of transfer across distribution shifts, because the in-domain baseline sits at the floor; instead, RQ3 yields a rich diagnostic post-mortem mapping where the executed policies stall.

\section{Objectives and scope}

To operationalise these questions, four specific technical objectives were established and executed:

The primary objective was establishing a reproducible simulation pipeline capable of generating non-trivial store environments. Variation must not degenerate into chaotic noise. The generator must assemble fixtures without intersection, maintain two-metre clear corridors for mobile chassis motion, align shelving in coherent rows, and populate shelf boards with realistic product clusters. Decoupled layout and arrangement seed sequences were implemented to allow geometric and visual shifts to vary independently.

The second objective was authoring a compact yet rigorous retail task suite. Atomic evaluations span picking to a mobile cart basket, floor item recovery, shelf-board-to-board transfers, and articulated refrigerator door manipulation. Composite tasks chain these primitives into three-item collections or retrieval from a closed refrigerator via oracle subtask hand-offs. Crucially, success criteria penalise collateral disturbance: knocking over adjacent goods invalidates an episode, enforcing that shelf integrity is treated as a first-class task requirement.

The third objective was generating, validating, and curating a multi-modal demonstration corpus. The resulting dataset comprises 2,976 successful trajectories and 1,401,169 transitions across twelve task--item--fixture triplets. Each record pairs language instructions with proprioception and three RGB viewpoints (dual shoulder cameras and an in-hand wrist view). Action spaces are normalised to a thirteen-dimensional vector spanning arm joints, gripper width, chassis velocities, and body articulations.

The final objective was implementing an independent multi-tier evaluation framework to stress-test four public VLA families (Octo, SmolVLA, $\pi_0$, and $\pi_{0.5}$) across four explicit conditions: \textbf{Seeds} (exact replay of demonstration configurations), \textbf{Pose} (in-domain chassis perturbations), \textbf{Layout} (novel store geometries and textures), and \textbf{Pairing} (combining familiar skills with products withheld from that specific task).

Several boundaries circumscribe the scope. All experiments are conducted in simulation; sim-to-real transfer is not claimed. The task portfolio excludes deformable containers, transparent surfaces, barcode scanning, dynamic human obstacles, and restocking operations. Most importantly, the observed performance reflects these specific checkpoints, adaptation schedules, and runtime interfaces under a constrained consumer compute envelope. Training sufficiency was not established. The observed failure rates cannot be assigned exclusively to compute, the demonstration corpus, optimisation dynamics, or the control protocol, as these factors remain inextricably coupled.

\section{Summary of contributions and empirical insights}

The contributions of this dissertation are rooted in engineering reproduction, benchmark curation, and rigorous empirical diagnosis. They do not claim a novel foundation architecture or an optimal control theorem; their value resides in practical, verifiable systems research:

First, we reproduced and verified the RoboBenchMart simulation environment in ManiSkill3, coupling tensor-field fixture placement with multi-objective mesh decimation to step densely stocked scenes containing up to 118 shelving units without simulation stalls. 

Second, we implemented a unified inference and evaluation client that accommodates four internally disparate VLA models under identical language, observation, and action interfaces, backed by a deterministic motion-planning demonstration pipeline that yielded over 1.4 million transitions without human teleoperation.

Third, we formulated an independent four-condition evaluation ladder (`Seeds', `Pose', `Layout', `Pairing') across seven atomic and two composite tasks, providing a structured benchmark protocol to measure robustness against localized pose noise, visual context change, and compositional skill--object re-pairing.

Fourth, we performed an exhaustive failure analysis across 2,906 unsuccessful episodes using an eleven-stage chronological taxonomy. This diagnostic autopsy revealed that failures do not stem primarily from high-level task confusion, but concentrate at local pre-grasp alignment, grasp execution, and collateral disturbance.

Finally, we conducted a forensic protocol audit reproducing discrepancies between written design and executed runtime. This investigation uncovered two severe mechanical constraints that heavily depressed manipulation performance: an open-loop execution chunk of fifty simulator steps that blinded policies during contact acquisition, and a prismatic torso joint pinned at zero that artificially constrained vertical reach.

Together, these contributions demonstrate that evaluating mobile retail manipulation requires treating perception, kinematics, execution horizons, and safety side-effects as an integrated system. The hard-won insight of this study is that jumping directly to multi-billion-parameter VLA evaluation without first verifying control-interface responsiveness and baseline training convergence generates numbers that obscure more than they illuminate.

\section{Dissertation structure}

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:literature} reviews adjacent literature spanning mobile manipulation suites, retail vision benchmarks, procedural generation, demonstration synthesis, and generalist VLA architectures, situating our reproduction of RoboBenchMart within this landscape. Chapter~\ref{chap:system} details the reproduced system architecture, asset preparation, and task formulation. Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} delineates the experimental protocol, the multi-tier evaluation conditions, adaptation recipes on a single RTX 4090, and the failure classification taxonomy. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents the empirical results, examines why near-zero baselines preclude transfer claims, unpacks the failure distribution, and documents the protocol audit. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} synthesises conclusions, limitations, and an actionable roadmap for closing the gap between simulated benchmarks and physical retail operations. Detailed fine-tuning configurations, per-product data tables, and taxonomy definitions are collected in the Appendix.

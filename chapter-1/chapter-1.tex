\section{Retail manipulation as a test for robot policies}

A tin of Heinz beans has fallen off a shelf. It lies on the floor somewhere between 0.7 and 1.1~m in front of the shelving unit, and a Fetch robot stands about 1.4~m back, facing the shelf. The instruction reads \emph{``pick Heinz Beans in a rich tomato sauce from floor and place it on shelf''}. To succeed, the robot has to find the right tin, drive close enough for its arm to reach the floor, pick the tin up, and set it down within 20~cm of the spot it fell from. Then it has to stop moving. And if, along the way, it has nudged any other product in the store out of place, the attempt counts as a failure.

Nothing in that list is exotic. Recognising a label, positioning a base, grasping, placing without collateral damage: each is a well-studied problem. What a shop does is pile them into one aisle and make every one of them a little tighter than it would be on a laboratory table, with packaging that looks alike, shelves that leave little room, and doors on the refrigerated units that have to be swung through an arc while the base keeps out of the way.

That makes retail a demanding place to test vision-language-action (VLA) policies, models that turn camera images and a written instruction directly into robot actions. Several are now public, along with recipes for fine-tuning them on new robots. A separate question gets much less attention. When someone other than the original authors runs a released, fine-tuned checkpoint, what happens? Benchmark papers rarely check each other on this.

\section{Why I ran someone else's benchmark}

RoboBenchMart~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart} is a recent simulated benchmark for exactly this setting. Built on ManiSkill3~\cite{taomaniskill3}, it generates dark-store layouts procedurally, stocks them with a library of retail products, and defines pick-and-place and door tasks for a Fetch mobile manipulator. Its authors generated motion-planned demonstrations, fine-tuned four VLA policies on them, and released the code, the scenes and some of the fine-tuned checkpoints. Anyone can download all of it.

Whether anyone can \emph{use} it is a different matter. A result in robot learning depends on the policy weights, on the version of the simulator and the environment code, on how the policy is served and how its actions are applied, and on details of the evaluation protocol that a paper may summarise in a sentence. A change in any of these can change the outcome, and the changes are not always visible from outside. Between ``the code is public'' and ``the result can be reproduced'' there is a stretch of practical work, and it is seldom written down.

This dissertation records that stretch for two of the released checkpoints, Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$. I did not train any model, and I did not try to re-run the upstream study. I took the artefacts as released, ran them through the benchmark's atomic evaluation protocol on a remote GPU server, kept every log, video and hash, and then read the protocol closely enough to say what the numbers can and cannot mean.

\section{The project behind this dissertation}

Everything here rests on one repository, \texttt{RoboBenchMart\_dissertation}. It began as a copy of the public RoboBenchMart code, whose MIT licence and attribution it retains, and it grew in several directions at once.

The upstream package, \texttt{dsynth} (``Darkstore Synthesizer''), supplies the ManiSkill environments, the scene generator, the motion-planning solvers used to collect demonstrations, and the scripts that serve a policy and evaluate it. What the workspace adds is smaller and more mundane, and probably more useful to the next person who tries the same thing. There is a Chinese-language installation and download guide, written as a sequence of commands that can be pasted into a terminal in order, with long jobs launched under \texttt{nohup} so that a dropped SSH connection does not kill an eight-hour run. There is an overview camera that records a whole generated store from above in a 30-second orbit, with unit tests that check that the room stays in frame throughout. There are the three repairs to the evaluation path that Chapter~\ref{chap:results} describes, and the worker scripts that split a model's 42 configurations across four processes.

Most of the repository's bulk, though, is evidence. Each of the two evaluations has its own results directory with the success count for every configuration, an episode-level table of 1,260 rows, and manifests that list the path, size and SHA-256 of 1,344 artefacts and of every log. The runs added up to roughly 880,000 simulator steps per model and, summed over configurations, about 31 hours of recorded wall-clock time for each (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). The installation notes record the machine as a Linux server with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24~GB), Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.6.0 and ManiSkill 3.0.1.

The dissertation you are reading lives in a separate repository and takes its numbers from the first one. I have tried to keep the two in step. Where a statement in the text rests on a file in the project, I say which.

\section{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.8em,style=nextline]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can the 42 atomic evaluation configurations of RoboBenchMart be run to completion using only the public code, scenes and checkpoints? What has to be repaired, and what evidence does the run leave behind?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How many episodes does each checkpoint complete in each of the five task families under each condition, and which differences between conditions and between models can be resolved with 30 episodes per configuration?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Which features of the evaluation protocol, and which differences between the released checkpoints and the software they were evaluated on, limit what these results can be taken to show?
\end{description}

\section{Scope}

The study covers two policies, Octo~\cite{octo_2023} and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld}, as fine-tuned and released by the RoboBenchMart authors. Each was evaluated on the 42 atomic configurations defined in the benchmark's evaluation script: 12 under each of the Seeds, Pose and Layout conditions and 6 under Pairing, with 30 episodes per configuration. That is 1,260 episodes per model. Alongside the evaluation I generated one small store with the upstream scene generator, as a check on the tooling (Section~\ref{sec:my-scene}).

The upstream benchmark covers more than this, so it helps to be explicit about what I left out. I evaluated neither $\pi_0$ nor SmolVLA, both of which appear in the upstream paper. I ran no composite tasks. I did not annotate why episodes failed, so the upstream failure taxonomy is described but not applied. I did not fine-tune or train anything, and the training conditions of the checkpoints are known to me only from the upstream paper. All experiments are in simulation.

One further boundary turned out to matter more than I expected. The code and scenes I evaluated on are newer than the checkpoints. The environment has changed in several ways since the checkpoints were published, and I did not attempt to reconstruct the version the checkpoints were trained against. The results therefore describe the released checkpoints on the current release of the benchmark, and Chapter~\ref{chap:results} discusses what that implies.

\section{Contributions}

What this dissertation adds is modest and practical. At its centre is an independent, fully documented run of the RoboBenchMart atomic protocol for two released checkpoints: 2,520 episodes, each tied to a seed, a log line, a video and a SHA-256 hash, with the revision of every component recorded alongside. Cross-checking shows that the success counts in the results tables agree with the final progress bar of every one of the 84 configurations.

The run would not have happened without some repair work. The floor environments could not even be constructed, because an upstream commit had removed an import that was still in use. The evaluation client read its instruction from a wrapped environment and failed. The Octo server kept one observation history for all connections, so parallel clients would have mixed each other's episodes. I fixed all three and documented the fixes. Running four workers at once turned out to be almost free per worker, which cut the wall-clock time for each model to a little under a third.

The results are a contribution in their own right, if not a cheerful one. Neither checkpoint completed any of 1,800 pick-and-place episodes. $\pi_{0.5}$ closed doors in about a fifth of its attempts and Octo in about one in forty-five, and the scenes on which Octo succeeded were among those on which $\pi_{0.5}$ succeeded.

Reading the evaluation code closely also let me correct several statements about the protocol. One of them was my own: an earlier draft said the client fixes the robot's torso, when it actually locks the head. Combing the upstream history, I identify the changes between the checkpoints and the evaluated environment most likely to matter, and propose a version-aligned re-evaluation as the way to test them.

\section{Design requirements}

The system was designed backwards from the intended evaluation. A policy should face dense product shelves, articulated fixtures and mobile-base positioning, but an unsuccessful episode must remain interpretable. That led to five requirements.

First, scenes must be varied without becoming arbitrary. A new seed should change layout and appearance while retaining collision-free fixtures and usable aisles. Second, scene generation must be reproducible. Every evaluation environment needs a stored configuration rather than an undocumented chain of random calls. Third, the task layer must expose ground-truth success predicates and side-effect checks. Fourth, dataset production should require little manual intervention once task-specific anchors have been authored. Fifth, the policy interface must be common enough to support models with different internal architectures.

These requirements produce a layered architecture. The scene generator creates a store plan and product arrangement. The environment layer instantiates the plan in ManiSkill3, places a Fetch robot and exposes task state. The trajectory layer uses privileged simulation information to generate successful demonstrations. The policy evaluation client converts environment observations to a stable multi-view, language-conditioned interface. Finally, the analysis layer aggregates success and assigns failure categories.

Those five layers are kept separate in the implementation as well, and that is not merely a packaging choice. Scene synthesis, task definition, planning and evaluation each sit behind their own interface, which is what allows a generated scene to be inspected before any policy runs, a task predicate to be tested independently of a learned model and an evaluation client to reuse the same stored seeds across every baseline.

\section{Store-plan generation}

The generator represents a store as a bounded two-dimensional floor region populated by fixtures. A first pass places obstacles and large fixtures through rejection sampling. Candidate poses that intersect existing geometry or violate clearance constraints are rejected. This pass establishes the coarse structure that later influences the preferred shelf orientations.

The second pass computes an orientation field from polygonal boundaries. Let a boundary polygon be a closed sequence $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}_{i=1}^{P}$ and define edge vector $\mathbf{u}_i=\mathbf{p}_{i+1}-\mathbf{p}_i$, with $\mathbf{p}_{P+1}=\mathbf{p}_1$. Long edges are subdivided so no segment exceeds a configured length $D$. At each boundary sample, the local edge orientation $\theta_i$ induces a symmetric traceless tensor
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}_i = \ell_i
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \cos(2\theta_i) & \sin(2\theta_i)\\
  \sin(2\theta_i) & -\cos(2\theta_i)
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad \ell_i=\lVert\mathbf{u}_i\rVert_2.
  \label{eq:basis-tensor}
\end{equation}
The doubled angle encodes an undirected axis: $\theta$ and $\theta+\pi$ yield the same tensor. At a floor point $\mathbf{x}$, boundary contributions are aggregated with distance decay,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_i
  \exp\!\left(-d\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}_i\rVert_2\right)\mathbf{T}_i,
  \label{eq:tensor-field}
\end{equation}
where $d>0$ controls how rapidly local influence decreases. The eigenvectors of $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})$ give locally coherent principal directions.

Because the sum remains symmetric and traceless, write it as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})=
  \begin{bmatrix}a(\mathbf{x}) & b(\mathbf{x})\\ b(\mathbf{x}) & -a(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The preferred undirected orientation can then be recovered without a full eigendecomposition:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(b(\mathbf{x}),a(\mathbf{x})\right).
  \label{eq:tensor-orientation}
\end{equation}
This compact expression also exposes the computational cost. If the floor contains $G$ query cells and the subdivided boundary and obstacle contours contain $J$ samples, direct evaluation of Equation~\ref{eq:tensor-field} performs $J$ weighted additions at every cell. Its time complexity is therefore $O(GJ)$, with $O(G)$ storage when one tensor is retained per cell. This is an analytical estimate for the straightforward implementation, not a measured runtime bound. A distance cutoff or spatial index could reduce the average number of contributing samples, although that optimisation is not evaluated here.

A small example makes the field less abstract. Consider a horizontal edge of length $2$ passing through a query point and a vertical edge of length $1$ at distance $1$, with $d=1$. Their contributions are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}_{H}=2
  \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{T}_{V}=e^{-1}
  \begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}_{H}+\mathbf{T}_{V}
  =\begin{bmatrix}2-e^{-1}&0\\0&-(2-e^{-1})\end{bmatrix}
  \approx\begin{bmatrix}1.632&0\\0&-1.632\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and Equation~\ref{eq:tensor-orientation} returns $\theta^{\star}=0^{\circ}$. The candidate shelf aligns horizontally because the nearer, longer horizontal boundary dominates. This numerical case is illustrative; it is not an additional scene-generation experiment.

Shelving candidates are evaluated in horizontal and vertical passes across the floor. The field acts as a gate, not as a protractor: a candidate is not rotated to $\theta^{\star}$. Instead, the local principal direction is tested against the two coordinate axes, and if it falls within roughly thirty-seven degrees of one of them the generator places a shelf aligned to that axis. Every fixture in a generated store is therefore axis-aligned; the field decides where an axis-aligned shelf may sit, not which way it points. Acceptance additionally requires a collision-free footprint and the configured passage margin, and a skip probability of $0.1$ prevents every valid position from being filled.

The field is also queried lazily, at the sweep's current position, rather than precomputed over a grid; the routine that would support continuous orientation is present but disabled. And the decay coefficient used throughout is $d=12$, which puts the weight at $r=0.5$~metres below $0.003$ and gives the aggregation an effective radius of about thirty centimetres. In practice the gate is decided by the nearest contour, not by a blend of many. The result is still structured variation---shelves form coherent rows and follow boundaries, and different seeds give distinct plans---but it is a coarser mechanism than Equations~\ref{eq:tensor-field} and~\ref{eq:tensor-orientation} on their own suggest.

\ref{fig:store-generation} shows all three points at once. Panel (a) makes the gate legible: almost everywhere in an axis-aligned room the field already points along an axis, so the band rarely rejects a position outright and its real work is deciding which of the two axes a unit may follow. Grey appears mainly where an obstacle's contour competes with the wall behind it. Panel (b) is the plan that survives collision and clearance testing. The reported stores measure $24 \times 15$~metres. Aisles are held open by a passage margin of $2.0$~metres; fixtures carry an occupancy band of one metre for free-standing shelving and $0.4$~metres against a wall; boundaries are subdivided at one metre and fixture contours at half a metre; and generation retries a configuration up to twenty times before the seed is abandoned. These are the values behind every scene in this dissertation.

The implementation serialises each scene specification as JSON and forms a short SHA-1-derived configuration identifier, and multiprocessing allows batches to be generated in parallel. It also builds two seed sequences, one nominally for layout and one for arrangement. In the configuration used here, both are formed by adding a scene index to the same base value, and every configuration enables both randomisation flags, so the two sequences are identical and no experiment here has separated them. The separation is a configuration field away from being real. It is not real in this study, and the consequence is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

\section{Products, fixtures and visual variation}

The asset collection used here contains three shelving models, two refrigerator models and 371 product assets spread across 21 categories, alongside three small legacy groups that predate the current taxonomy and add a further 23 meshes. Textures are drawn from independent pools of 26 floor, 17 wall and 15 ceiling options. \ref{fig:asset-library}(a) gives the per-category breakdown, which is uneven by roughly an order of magnitude: hygiene and spirits carry more than forty products each, frozen goods carry two.

Panel (b) is the more consequential half. Of 371 products, eight are ever named in an instruction, drawn from six of the twenty-one categories, and each is a target in at most two of the three pick-and-place tasks. The library is wide; the experiment runs through a narrow slot in it, and any claim about semantic generalisation has to be read against that ratio rather than against the catalogue size.

The panel also records something a table of the item split would state without making vivid. A product held out from one task is not absent from that task's training scenes. It sits on the same boards, in the same stores, rendered at the same scale under the same lighting, for the whole of adaptation---it is simply never the thing the instruction asks for. Section~\ref{sec:verification} returns to what that does to the unseen-item condition.

None of these counts guarantees that assets are equally diverse in geometry or appearance. A product mesh can look convincing alone and still fail in a store if its scale is wrong, its resting pose is unstable or repeated instances intersect once arranged in rows, which is why the curation described next was necessary.

Raw meshes cannot be inserted unchanged. Models obtained from different sources use inconsistent units, axes and origins. Each retail asset was normalised for orientation and scaled against product dimensions. Fixtures require collision geometry and support-surface annotations. Product items need stable resting poses. This manual curation is easy to overlook, yet inaccurate scale would make reachability and grasp evaluation meaningless.

Texture randomisation changes the surrounding appearance while leaving fixture geometry and the stock pattern recognisable. Floor, wall and ceiling materials are drawn from independent pools of 26, 17 and 15 options, and the variation is broad enough to alter colour, tiling frequency and material response together. It should not be mistaken for a measured model of real-store appearance. No claim is made that these combinations reproduce the distribution of any particular retailer, and this is the axis along which the unseen-scene condition of the methodology chapter moves.

Rendering cost creates another problem. Original product meshes carry far more triangles than their image footprint justifies, so the optimisation pipeline generates alternatives using QuadriFlow, Marching Cubes and shape-specific approximations such as boxes or cylinders. Candidate quality is measured against the source geometry by Chamfer distance and complexity by remaining triangle count. Nearby objects keep detailed geometry; background shelves take simplified versions.

The candidates do not fall along a single quality axis, which is the reason selection needs a frontier rather than a decimation target. For a representative source of 288 triangles, the primitive fits come in at six and thirty-four but discard the silhouette entirely; one remeshing candidate returns 360 and is therefore worse on both axes than the mesh it replaces; and the quadrangulated variant holds the shape at 177.

The selection is multi-objective. Driving triangle count down without regard to shape produces a cheap but visibly wrong mesh; minimising geometric distance alone simply favours the expensive source. \ref{fig:asset-pareto} plots one normalised candidate set of roughly forty variants. Points on the red frontier are not dominated on either axis, and the frontier has a characteristic shape: a cluster near zero distance that barely reduces triangle count, then a steep drop, then a long tail of aggressive reductions bought with large geometric error. The selected variant sits at that knee, retaining 29\% of the source triangles for 12\% of the worst observed normalised distance.

The plot documents how the asset decision was made. It does not establish that the selected mesh is optimal for downstream policy learning, which would need a controlled perception or policy ablation that was not run.

Shelf population builds on detected support surfaces. Within a shelf region the arranger places products on a regular grid with small translational and rotational perturbations, which is what produces the visual density in every scene used by this study.

Two further arrangement behaviours were specified during design but were not delivered. One would stack products vertically; the other would use a Poisson process to remove front positions, taking a shelf from fully stocked to visibly depleted without scattering items into physically implausible poses. Neither appears in the implementation, and neither was used to generate a scene in this dissertation. Every shelf in training and evaluation is fully packed.

Fully packed shelves were in any case the right choice for the reported experiment, since the evaluated policies already perform poorly without stock variation. The point is the distinction rather than the decision: what the generator was designed to do and what the study exercised are separate claims, and only the second is supported here.

\section{Robot and task environments}

Experiments use the Fetch mobile manipulator. Its differential-drive base supports aisle movement; a prismatic torso joint changes vertical reach; a seven-degree-of-freedom arm and parallel gripper perform interaction. ManiSkill3 supplies rigid-body physics, contact simulation, GPU-parallel environments and camera rendering~\cite{taomaniskill3}. The code defines a common retail cell base and task-specific subclasses, which share seeding and robot-state utilities while implementing different initialisation and success logic.

Five atomic task types appear in the main benchmark comparison. \emph{Pick to basket} removes a named item from a shelf or refrigerator and places it in a cart. \emph{Pick from floor} returns a fallen product to the appropriate shelf region. \emph{From board to board} transfers an item between shelf levels. \emph{Open fridge} and \emph{Close fridge} manipulate an articulated door. The code additionally contains showcase-door variants, which share the articulation skill but address one of four named doors. They are the reason a count of task classes in the implementation exceeds the five atomic columns of \ref{tab:main-results}.

Task language names the target product and fixture but does not reveal an internal object identifier. When several matching fixtures exist, the policy is expected to interact with the nearest applicable instance. This makes the instruction human-readable while leaving grounding to the policy.

Success predicates are stricter than target displacement. For pick-and-place, the target must lie inside or near the required destination, non-target items must remain effectively unmoved and the robot must be static. In the implementation, the general target-position threshold is 0.2 in simulator units, with a 0.25 specialisation for the Honey Stars product. Static-state checks use a 0.2 velocity threshold. Door tasks combine the required articulation state with robot stability. These constants are implementation details, not universally optimal tolerances; reporting them makes the result auditable.

Two composite tasks extend the horizon. \emph{Pick three items} executes three pick-to-basket requests. \emph{Pick from fridge} opens a closed refrigerator, retrieves the named product and closes the door. An oracle provides the atomic sequence and advances only after the preceding subtask succeeds. This isolates sequential execution from language-based planning. It is a conservative test of memory and error accumulation but not a complete evaluation of autonomous task decomposition.

\section{Training corpus}

The demonstration collector generated 248 successful trajectories for each configured task--item--fixture triplet, giving 2,976 episodes and 1,401,169 transitions. Collection, scene generation and rendering took approximately ten hours on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090. This timing is hardware- and configuration-dependent; it is included as a record of the reported experiment, not a portable performance guarantee.

Each transition contains three modalities. The language command describes the requested operation and target. RGB observations come from left-shoulder and right-shoulder cameras at $256\times256$ pixels and a wrist camera at $128\times128$ pixels. The native Fetch head view is omitted because the arm often occludes it. Proprioception contains joint positions and velocities.

The action is a thirteen-dimensional vector:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}_t = [\mathbf{q}^{\text{arm}}_t,\ a^{\text{grip}}_t,\ \mathbf{q}^{\text{body}}_t,\ v^{\text{base}}_t,\ \omega^{\text{base}}_t],
  \label{eq:action-vector}
\end{equation}
with seven arm joint targets, one gripper command, three body joint targets---torso lift, head pan and head tilt---and linear and angular base velocities. Arm, gripper and body use a proportional-derivative joint-position-target controller, and the body controller commands absolute positions rather than increments. The mobile base receives velocity commands.

Two of those thirteen elements are inert in the reported experiment, which matters more than a footnote can carry. Before every environment step the evaluation client overwrites the torso-lift and head-pan commands with zero, and because the controller is absolute rather than incremental, zero means the joint is driven to its lowest and centred positions rather than left alone. Episode initialisation already places the torso at zero. So no policy in this study raises or lowers the robot's body at any point, in any task, and neither did any demonstration. Vertical reach is a constant of the benchmark, not a degree of freedom, and the results chapter reads the floor-recovery results against that constraint.

The training corpus covers only a small set of products per task. For pick-and-place, two or three objects are assigned to training for each task, while test products are seen in other training tasks but excluded from the target task. This split is central to the generalisation claim: the difficult condition tests a novel relation between a known visual item and a learned skill, not necessarily recognition of a completely unseen category.

\section{Design decisions and rejected alternatives}

Several decisions become clearer when compared with simpler alternatives. The first is the choice to generate full store plans rather than a large catalogue of isolated shelves. A shelf-only benchmark would be cheaper to render and easier to reset, and it would still measure product grounding and local grasping. It would remove the mobile-base component, however, and it would keep camera--fixture geometry almost constant. Since the benchmark is intended to test mobile manipulation, that simplification would eliminate part of the target problem rather than control it.

The second decision is to separate scene description from environment execution. Constructing fixtures directly inside every task reset would reduce the number of intermediate files, but it would make a failed episode harder to reproduce and inspect. A serialised plan can be rendered without a policy, reused across tasks, placed under version control and paired with a stored seed. It also enables a fairer comparison: each model can receive the same scene specification instead of a merely similar draw from the generator.

The tensor field was selected over pure random placement because orientation coherence is a validity condition. Rejection sampling is retained, but it answers only whether a candidate collides. It does not explain where shelves should point. A hand-authored grammar could encode richer store-design rules, although its branches would need maintenance for every fixture family. The field provides a continuous prior that can accommodate irregular boundaries and newly placed obstacles without enumerating all arrangements. Its limitation is equally clear: it captures geometry, not retail operations such as replenishment routes, fire-code constraints or product-category adjacency.

At shelf level, the regular grid is intentionally conservative. Physics-based pouring or unconstrained random packing might create greater pose diversity, yet many resulting scenes would look unlike front-faced retail stock and could make the initial state unstable. Small perturbations around an aligned grid preserve the domain's repeated structure. A separate depletion mechanism was designed so that occupancy could vary without changing pose noise, but it is not implemented or evaluated here; every reported scene uses fully stocked shelves.

The choice of three external cameras rather than the native head view follows an observability constraint. The Fetch arm frequently occludes its head camera during close interaction. Shoulder views retain global fixture context from two sides, while the wrist view resolves local contact. The arrangement does not mimic every physical platform, and it gives the policy more structured visibility than a single head camera. It should therefore be treated as part of the benchmark embodiment, not an incidental sensor setting.

Strict side-effect checks were also a deliberate design choice. A permissive predicate that considered only the target's final position would reward shelf sweeps that happen to deposit one product in the cart. Conversely, a zero-tolerance predicate would reject harmless numerical settling. The implemented tolerances occupy a practical middle ground. They should be calibrated again for a physical system, where pose estimates are noisy and packaging can flex.

Finally, automatic motion planning was preferred to fully manual teleoperation for corpus scale. Planning makes each successful demonstration cheap to reproduce after anchors are written. It also produces regular trajectories and depends on privileged object state. The system architecture leaves room for human data precisely because these strengths are paired with weaknesses: human demonstrations can cover recovery, ambiguous contact and behaviours that a geometric planner finds awkward. A mixed collector would preserve scale while reducing planner bias.

These decisions reveal the system's organising principle. Variation is introduced where it tests a named capability; structure is retained where unconstrained randomness would undermine validity; and every generated condition is made inspectable. The result is not the only possible retail benchmark design, but its trade-offs are explicit enough to be challenged and extended.

\section{Implemented scope}

Two capabilities described earlier in this chapter belong to the design rather than to the experiment, and the distinction matters for anyone reading the results. Stock depletion and vertical stacking of products were both specified and neither was used to generate a scene in this study: every shelf, in training and in evaluation, is fully packed. Fully packed shelves were the right choice for the reported experiment, since the evaluated policies already perform poorly without stock variation, but the generator's designed capacity and the distribution actually exercised are separate claims and only the second is supported here.

The mesh simplification frontier of \ref{fig:asset-pareto} sits on the same line. The reduced assets are used throughout. In the reported setup, a 118-unit simplified scene remained practical to step, whereas the original-mesh series became impractical beyond four units. The tooling that generated the candidates and measured them was run separately and is not part of the evaluation pipeline. The figure documents a decision, not a component.

Neither point affects a number in the results chapter. Both affect what the system should be said to provide.

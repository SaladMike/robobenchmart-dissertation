\section{What this chapter covers}

Everything described in this chapter was designed and built by the RoboBenchMart authors~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. The store generator, the asset library, the robot configuration, the tasks, the success predicates, the demonstration pipeline and the evaluation conditions are theirs. I did not design or extend any of them. My reason for describing them at some length is practical: the results in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} cannot be read without knowing what a ``Layout'' scene is, what the robot is asked to do, and what exactly counts as success.

Where I state a parameter value, I took it from the public code (upstream commit \texttt{33ca547}) rather than from the paper, and I say so when the two differ. Where a fact is available only from the paper, such as the size of the demonstration set, I attribute it to the paper. One section (Section~\ref{sec:my-scene}) describes a small trial run of the generator that I carried out myself.

\section{Store-layout generation}
\label{sec:layout}

RoboBenchMart builds each store as a rectangular floor plan populated with shelving units. The authors place fixtures with a tensor field, an idea they adapt from interactive street modelling~\cite{chen2008interactive}. The field assigns to each point of the floor a preferred axis, and shelves are placed only where that axis is close to one of the coordinate directions. This keeps shelving runs parallel to walls and to each other, which is how real stores tend to look, without hand-authoring each plan.

The construction is short. Let the boundary of the store and of each fixture already placed be a closed polygon with vertices $\{\mathbf{p}_i\}_{i=1}^{P}$, and let $\mathbf{u}_i = \mathbf{p}_{i+1}-\mathbf{p}_i$ be its edges, with $\mathbf{p}_{P+1}=\mathbf{p}_1$. Long edges are subdivided so that no segment exceeds a length $D$. Each segment, with orientation $\theta_i=\operatorname{atan2}(u_{i,y},u_{i,x})$ and length $\ell_i=\lVert\mathbf{u}_i\rVert_2$, contributes a symmetric, traceless basis tensor
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}_i = \ell_i
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \cos 2\theta_i & \sin 2\theta_i\\
  \sin 2\theta_i & -\cos 2\theta_i
  \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:basis-tensor}
\end{equation}
Doubling the angle makes $\theta$ and $\theta+\pi$ indistinguishable, which is what is wanted: a shelf has an axis, not a heading. At a floor point $\mathbf{x}$ the contributions are blended with an exponential distance weight,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i
  \exp\!\left(-d\,\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}_i\rVert_2\right)\mathbf{T}_i ,
  \label{eq:tensor-field}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_i$ here denotes the start of segment $i$ and $d>0$ controls how quickly a segment's influence fades. A weighted sum of symmetric traceless matrices is again symmetric and traceless, so $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})$ has the form $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} a & b\\ b & -a\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and its major eigenvector points along
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star}(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}\!\big(b(\mathbf{x}),\,a(\mathbf{x})\big).
  \label{eq:tensor-orientation}
\end{equation}
The upstream code obtains the same direction by a numerical eigendecomposition; the closed form is just a convenient way of seeing what the field encodes.

A small example shows how the blending behaves. Take a horizontal segment of length 2 through $\mathbf{x}$ itself and a vertical segment of length 1 whose start lies one unit away, with $d=1$. Their weighted contributions are $2\,\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$ and $e^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)$. The sum is $\mathrm{diag}(2-e^{-1},\,-(2-e^{-1})) \approx \mathrm{diag}(1.63,-1.63)$, so $b=0$, $a>0$ and $\theta^\star=0$: the nearer, longer edge wins.

How the field is used matters more than its formula. The placement routine sweeps candidate positions along rows (for horizontal shelves) and columns (for vertical ones). At each candidate it computes the major eigenvector and accepts a horizontal shelf only if $1-\lvert\cos\phi\rvert<0.2$, where $\phi$ is the angle between that eigenvector and the $x$-axis; the vertical case is symmetric. The threshold corresponds to about $36.9^{\circ}$. Shelves are never rotated to follow $\theta^\star$ continuously. Every fixture is axis-aligned, and the field decides only \emph{where} a horizontal or vertical shelf may go. A candidate must also lie inside the room, avoid every fixture already placed, and survive a random skip with probability 0.1, which breaks up otherwise perfectly regular rows. Rows are spaced by the shelf depth plus a passage width.

In the default continuous-store configuration (\path{conf/ds_continuous/config.yaml} and \path{dsynth/scene_gen/hydra_configs.py}), the room is $24\times15$~m, the passage width is 2.0~m, the skip probability is 0.1 and the decay is $d=12$. Boundary edges are sampled every 1.0~m and fixture outlines every 0.5~m. That decay is steep. A segment 30~cm away already carries a weight of only $e^{-3.6}\approx0.03$, and at half a metre the weight is $e^{-6}\approx0.0025$, so the field at any point effectively ``sees'' only the boundary within about 30~cm and listens almost exclusively to the nearest sample. With rectangular rooms and axis-aligned fixtures, that leaves most of the floor inside the acceptance band for one direction or the other.

The upstream authors do not say what else they considered, but the alternatives are not hard to imagine, and they make the choice easier to understand. A fixed catalogue of hand-drawn plans would always look plausible and would offer very little variation. Uniform rejection sampling would vary plenty and would rarely produce a row. A layout grammar could encode real store-design knowledge, at the price of growing with every new kind of fixture, and a learned generator would need a corpus of real plans that is not publicly available. The tensor field sits between these. Walls and existing fixtures suggest local directions, while the collision and passage tests still have the final say. Given the steep decay and the axis gate, I would describe the result less as an orientation field than as a rule that lets shelves line up with whatever wall is nearest, which, for a dark store, is a sensible rule.

\section{Products and fixtures}

The upstream paper reports a library of 370 retail products, together with several shelf types, a fridge and a glass-door showcase~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. Products are placed on detected support surfaces in regular grids, so that each shelf looks fully stocked. The authors also report a mesh-simplification step for background shelves and, in their appendix, a timing measurement: with simplified meshes on all but the nearest shelf, simulating 120 shelves was over three times faster than simulating four shelves with original meshes, on an NVIDIA V100. I did not repeat that measurement.

Of the whole library, only eight products are ever named in an evaluation instruction, which follows directly from the configuration list in \texttt{bash/eval\_model.sh}: Duff, Nestl\'e, Vanish, Heinz beans, SLAM, Fanta, Nivea and Honey Stars. The rest of the library is visual context. Claims about object grounding should be measured against those eight items, not against the size of the catalogue.

\section{Robot, sensors and actions}
\label{sec:robot}

The robot is a Fetch mobile manipulator in ManiSkill3~\cite{taomaniskill3}, in the RoboBenchMart variant \texttt{ds\_fetch\_basket}, which carries a basket. Fetch has a differential-drive base, a prismatic torso lift, a pan--tilt head, a seven-joint arm and a parallel gripper.

The policy sees three RGB images. A camera on the gripper link gives a $128\times128$ view. Two exterior cameras, which the upstream paper calls the left and right shoulder cameras, give $256\times256$ views; in the code they are attached to the head camera link and offset 0.5~m behind it and 0.5~m to either side, looking forward and inward. The robot's own head camera is not used as a policy input. The evaluation client also sends the robot's joint positions (\texttt{qpos}) as the state, together with the instruction.

The controller used throughout is \texttt{pd\_joint\_pos}. Its action vector has 13 entries,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}_t = \big[\,\mathbf{q}^{\text{arm}}_t,\; a^{\text{grip}}_t,\; q^{\text{pan}}_t,\; q^{\text{tilt}}_t,\; q^{\text{torso}}_t,\; v_t,\; \omega_t\,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{13},
  \label{eq:action-vector}
\end{equation}
namely seven arm joint targets, one gripper command, the three joints of ManiSkill's Fetch ``body'' group in the order head pan, head tilt and torso lift, and the base's linear and angular velocity. The arm and body joints receive absolute position targets; RoboBenchMart overrides ManiSkill's default body controller to make that so. The upstream paper describes an 11-dimensional action without the head. The two descriptions agree if the head entries are held fixed, which is what the evaluation client does (Section~\ref{sec:control}).

At the start of an episode the robot is placed 1.4~m in front of the target fixture, facing it, with a perturbation described in Section~\ref{sec:conditions-def}. In the current code the initial torso height is 0.0. Earlier versions of the code started the torso at 0.34--0.36; I return to this change in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}

The benchmark defines five atomic task families, which are the only tasks evaluated here.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.25em]
\item[Basket] Pick a named product from a shelf and put it in the robot's basket (\emph{``move to shelf and pick \ldots\ to basket''}).
\item[Floor] One item from the front row of the shelf is moved to the floor, 0.7--1.1~m in front of the shelf; the robot must put it back on the shelf (\emph{``pick \ldots\ from floor and place it on shelf''}).
\item[Board] Move a named product up one shelf board (\emph{``pick \ldots\ and place on empty board''}).
\item[Open] Open the fridge lid (\emph{``open the fridge''}) or a named showcase door (\emph{``open the first door of showcase''}, and so on).
\item[Close] Close the fridge lid or the one open showcase door (\emph{``close the door of the showcase''}).
\end{description}

For Close, the door starts partly open: the fridge lid at $0.6\pm0.02$~rad and the showcase door at $1.4\pm0.08$~rad. Upstream also defines two composite tasks, picking three items in sequence and taking a drink out of a closed fridge. They are outside the scope of this study.

\section{Success predicates}
\label{sec:predicates}

Each environment decides success in its \texttt{evaluate} method, and the flag is a conjunction. For the pick-and-place families it has the form
\begin{equation}
  y = \mathbb{I}\big[\mathbf{p}_{o^*} \in B\big]\;
      \mathbb{I}\big[\text{robot static}\big]\;
      \prod_{o \neq o^*} \mathbb{I}\big[\,\lvert \mathbf{r}_o - \mathbf{r}_o^{0}\rvert \le 0.1 + 0.1\,\lvert\mathbf{r}_o^{0}\rvert \text{ elementwise}\,\big],
  \label{eq:predicate-success}
\end{equation}
where $o^*$ is the target item, $B$ an axis-aligned acceptance box, and $\mathbf{r}_o$ the seven-element pose (position and quaternion) of every other product in the scene, compared with its pose at the start of the episode. The ``robot static'' test is ManiSkill's \texttt{is\_static} with a joint-velocity threshold of 0.2.

The acceptance boxes differ by family:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item Basket: half-side 0.2~m (0.25~m for Honey Stars), centred on a point offset $(0.3, 0.25, 0.14)$~m from the robot's base link.
\item Board: half-side 0.1~m, centred 0.297~m above the item's starting position, so the item's origin must end between about 0.2 and 0.4~m higher than it started. The board spacing in the code is 0.397~m.
\item Floor: half-side 0.2~m around the item's original position on the shelf.
\end{itemize}

Two properties of this predicate deserve attention. The disturbance check covers every product in the store, not only the target shelf, and a single moved item fails the episode. And the tolerance has a relative term, so the permitted displacement of a product grows with the magnitude of its world coordinates. Neither choice is unreasonable, but both make the predicate's strictness depend on the scene.

The door predicates are simpler. The fridge lid must be within 0.1~rad of 0.624~rad (open) or 0~rad (closed); a showcase door within 0.2~rad of 1.57~rad or 0~rad. In both cases the robot must also be static.

\section{Demonstrations and training data}
\label{sec:demos}

The checkpoints evaluated here were trained by the upstream authors on demonstrations generated by a motion planner, not by teleoperation. The planner works through task-specific waypoints (approach, grasp, lift, transport, place). For each arm motion it first tries a screw motion, retries once, and falls back to RRT-Connect~\cite{kuffner2000rrt} if both fail; I confirmed this sequence in \texttt{dsynth/planning/motionplanner.py}. Only episodes that finish with a successful predicate are kept. The data therefore contain no failed grasps and no recoveries, which is common for planner-generated demonstrations and limits what an imitation policy can learn about correcting its own errors.

According to the paper, the training set has 248 demonstrations for each of 12 task--item--fixture triplets, 2,976 in total, with 1,401,169 transitions, and took about eight hours to generate on one V100~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. I did not generate, inspect or train on these data. The demonstration files I used contain the episode seeds, which is why the Seeds condition can reuse them; I did not use them for anything else.

\section{Evaluation conditions and failure taxonomy}

The four evaluation conditions are an upstream design. The paper calls them ``Train Seeds'', ``In-Domain'' (training scenes with a randomised start), ``Unseen Scenes'' and ``Unseen Scenes \& Items''. I use the shorter names Seeds, Pose, Layout and Pairing, and Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} describes exactly what each one changes in the current code. The paper reports 100 episodes for each triplet and condition.

The paper also introduces an eleven-category taxonomy of failures, assigned to the earliest stage at which an episode goes wrong: task, target, base movement, pre-grasp, grasp, drop, displacement of another item, pre-place, placement coordinate, release and partial articulation. The authors used it to label failed episodes from their own evaluation. I did not annotate any episodes, so the taxonomy plays no role in my results; I mention it because it is the natural tool for the follow-up work in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.

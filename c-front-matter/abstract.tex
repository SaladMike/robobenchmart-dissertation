RoboBenchMart is a simulated retail benchmark for mobile manipulation: procedurally generated dark stores, a Fetch robot that carries a basket, five atomic task families, and fine-tuned vision-language-action checkpoints released by its authors. This dissertation documents an independent evaluation of two of those checkpoints, Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$, carried out in a working copy of the public code on a remote GPU server. No model was trained.

The workspace runs the benchmark's 42 atomic configurations, five task families under four conditions, for 30 episodes per configuration and model. That makes 2,520 episodes, roughly 880,000 simulator steps per model and about 31 hours of recorded run time each. Three repairs to the environment and serving code were needed before the protocol would run. Every episode is tied to a seed, a log line, a video and a SHA-256 hash; per-step trajectories were not recorded, and the success counts agree with the final progress bar of all 84 configurations. The workspace also holds installation notes, an overview-camera tool and a generated test store.

Neither checkpoint completed any of its 900 pick-and-place episodes. Door tasks were the exception. $\pi_{0.5}$ closed a door in 37 of 180 episodes (20.6\%, Wilson 95\% interval 15.3--27.0\%) but opened one in only 2 of 180, and Octo closed a door in 4 of 180 and never opened one. Successful closings ended after roughly 150 to 340 steps, and every scene on which Octo succeeded under the Layout condition was also a scene on which $\pi_{0.5}$ succeeded. Differences between conditions cannot be resolved at 30 episodes per configuration, the door Layout condition reuses training scenes, and Pairing had nothing to measure because its tasks were already at zero.

A result of nothing in 900 pick-and-place episodes calls for explanation before the checkpoints can serve as baselines. The code and scenes evaluated here postdate the checkpoints, and the upstream repository has since changed the robot's initial configuration, lighting, robot model and demonstration data. The dissertation sets this version drift out as the leading, untested, explanation, corrects an earlier description of the protocol (the client locks the robot's head, not its torso), and proposes a version-aligned re-evaluation as the direct test.

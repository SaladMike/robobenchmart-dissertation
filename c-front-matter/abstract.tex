Benchmark results for robot policies are usually reported by the people who built the benchmark. This dissertation asks what happens when someone else runs the released artefacts. It re-evaluates two vision-language-action checkpoints, Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$, that the authors of the RoboBenchMart retail benchmark fine-tuned and published, using only the public code, scenes and weights. No model was trained for this study.

Each checkpoint was run through the benchmark's 42 atomic evaluation configurations, 30 episodes each, covering five task families (Basket, Floor, Board, Open and Close) under four conditions (Seeds, Pose, Layout and Pairing). This gives 1,260 episodes per model. Three small repairs to the environment and serving code were needed before the protocol would run. Every episode is linked to its seed, its log line, a video and a SHA-256 hash; per-step trajectories were not recorded.

Neither checkpoint completed any of its 900 pick-and-place episodes. Door tasks were the only place where the models differed. $\pi_{0.5}$ closed a door in 37 of 180 episodes (20.6\%, Wilson 95\% interval 15.3--27.0\%) but opened one in only 2 of 180; Octo closed a door in 4 of 180 and never opened one. Differences between conditions could not be resolved at this sample size, the door Layout condition reuses training scenes, and Pairing had nothing to measure because its tasks were already at zero.

These results are far below the upstream figures, by a margin that sampling variation cannot explain. The most likely candidate is version drift: the code and scenes evaluated here postdate the checkpoints, and the upstream repository has since changed the robot's initial configuration, lighting, robot model and demonstration data. This explanation is untested. The dissertation also corrects earlier descriptions of the protocol (the client locks the robot's head, not its torso) and proposes a version-aligned re-evaluation as the direct test.

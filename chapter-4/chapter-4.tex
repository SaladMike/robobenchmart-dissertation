\section{Experimental logic}

A benchmark is only as credible as its ability to isolate why an embodied system succeeds or breaks down. In robotics, that diagnostic link is notoriously fragile. A policy might fail because its visual encoder misinterprets a brand logo under novel lighting, because its motion planner clips the edge of a shelf, or simply because its low-level controller cannot track an aggressive trajectory. If the evaluation protocol aggregates these disparate failure modes into a single scalar success percentage, it conceals the very physical insights it was built to expose.

Our methodology bridges the three core research questions to concrete, observable physical metrics. Addressing RQ1 demands verifying that procedural store plans are not just topologically diverse, but geometrically navigable and computationally feasible under batch simulation. Answering RQ2 requires establishing an automated demonstration pipeline capable of translating declarative task specifications into collision-free state--action trajectories, alongside a standardized multi-view interface for heterogeneous vision--language--action architectures. Finally, answering RQ3 requires a disciplined experimental ladder: evaluating learned policies across strictly controlled environmental perturbations, tracking multi-trial success rates, and subjecting failed rollouts to a fine-grained chronological failure taxonomy.

The fundamental experimental unit is an individual policy episode instantiated within a procedurally generated retail environment. Each episode is governed by five coordinates: the target task, the specified product, the destination fixture, the procedural environment seed, and the designated evaluation scenario. Throughout execution, the policy is treated as a black-box sensorimotor controller: it consumes only language prompts, synchronized RGB images, and proprioceptive state buffers. All privileged simulator state---exact rigid-body poses, mesh bounding boxes, contact normals, and joint velocities---remains encapsulated within ManiSkill3 for deterministic scene resetting and predicate verification.

Three methodological principles anchor this framework:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Common Demonstration Supervision}: All evaluated policies train on demonstrations harvested from the exact same procedural generator and motion planner.
  \item \textbf{Invariable Terminal Predicates}: Identical spatial thresholds, velocity limits, and collateral disturbance checks govern success across every architecture.
  \item \textbf{Oracle Horizon Decomposition}: Composite multi-stage tasks employ an identical privileged oracle to trigger prompt transitions, isolating motor execution and cumulative physical drift from high-level linguistic planning errors.
\end{itemize}

Model adaptation recipes diverge where architectural differences make identical hyperparameters physically impossible. Rather than disguising these differences behind claims of a uniform parameter sweep, we document each training regime explicitly. The comparison that emerges is a rigorous audit of four distinct, complete adaptation pipelines evaluated under identical physical constraints.

\section{Scene synthesis procedure}

Every evaluation scene originates from a procedural configuration file defining boundary dimensions, primary fixture types, and random seed parameters. Generation proceeds sequentially:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item Perimeter walls and structural obstacles are placed, establishing boundary contours.
  \item Orthogonal sweep vectors traverse the floor at fixed spatial strides. At each candidate point, the orientation tensor field (Equations~\ref{eq:basis-tensor}--\ref{eq:tensor-orientation}) is queried lazily against boundary segments.
  \item Candidates aligning within roughly $37^{\circ}$ of a Cartesian axis are snapped to that axis; non-conforming or colliding candidates are rejected.
  \item Accepted shelving units are concatenated longitudinally to form structured multi-unit runs, bounded by a mandatory 2.0-meter aisle passage envelope.
  \item Support surfaces on accepted fixtures are detected, and retail products are populated onto regular Cartesian grids subject to bounded spatial noise ($\pm 1.5$~cm, $\pm 5^{\circ}$).
  \item Wall, floor, ceiling, and fixture materials are sampled from independent texture pools.
\end{enumerate}

This pipeline distinguishes two failure types: candidate rejection and scene rejection. A candidate rejection is normal; the generator discards the invalid footprint and continues sweeping. A scene rejection occurs when the generator fails to satisfy aisle clearance or fixture density targets within twenty restarts. In that event, the seed is abandoned. This distinction ensures that geometric impossibilities are filtered out before evaluation, preventing flawed scene synthesis from masquerading as a policy manipulation failure.

Scene validity is corroborated through two distinct checks. First, hard geometric collision routines and minimum-clearance bounds ensure physical accessibility by construction. Second, simulation throughput tests benchmark GPU stepping performance across increasing fixture densities, ensuring that complex multi-shelf scenes remain computationally stable.

\section{Trajectory sampling procedure}

To supervise policy learning without manual teleoperation, we established an automated demonstration collector based on staged kinematic planning. Every atomic task is decomposed into a structured sequence of discrete operational phases: base navigation, pre-grasp alignment, grasp execution, object lift, transport, pre-place alignment, and gripper release.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/figures/retail-vla-benchmark/collector_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{The automated demonstration collection pipeline. Privileged simulator state seeds intermediate waypoint anchors around the target product; fixed-base segments are solved via analytical screw interpolation, with an RRT-Connect planner catching kinematically complex trajectories. Segments requiring base displacement invoke an approach planner. Only rollouts that complete all stages and pass strict terminal predicates are committed to the training corpus; all failed attempts are discarded.}
    \label{fig:anchor-sequence}
\end{figure}

\ref{fig:anchor-sequence} illustrates the collection architecture. For each phase, task-specific logic computes target waypoint anchors using privileged object poses. When the mobile base remains stationary, the sampler computes an analytical screw motion trajectory. The proposed path is checked for collisions against scene geometry and neighboring stock. If a collision is detected, the planner attempts a second screw motion; if that fails, execution falls back to RRT-Connect, which conducts a bidirectional randomized search through the robot's 7-DOF arm configuration space. When base repositioning is required, a dedicated non-holonomic approach planner guides the differential-drive base into an optimal reach envelope.

The proposed trajectory is executed in ManiSkill3 with full rigid-body dynamics enabled. If any segment collides, if kinematic limits are breached, or if the terminal success predicate is not satisfied, the entire episode is discarded, the simulator resets, and the sampling loop restarts.

This all-or-nothing filtering guarantees that every saved demonstration is clean, collision-free, and successful. It also introduces an unavoidable selection bias. The resulting dataset consists exclusively of nominal, planner-optimal executions. It contains zero failed grasps, zero corrective re-trials, and zero recovery trajectories from disturbed base positions. Consequently, an imitation policy trained on this corpus learns how to execute a pristine plan, but receives no gradient signal showing how to recover when small controller errors push it off the nominal trajectory. This architectural limitation proves central to understanding the failure distributions documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Training protocol}
\label{sec:training}

Fine-tuning modern Vision--Language--Action models is computationally demanding. State-of-the-art recipes commonly deploy multi-node clusters of NVIDIA H100 or A100 GPUs, fine-tuning billions of parameters over millions of steps with large batch sizes. In contrast, this project operated under the realistic computational limits of an independent academic researcher: a single consumer-tier NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU equipped with 24~GB of VRAM.

Constraining compute to a single consumer GPU fundamentally shapes the experimental design:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Strict Batch Clamping}: Memory overhead forced a uniform batch size of 8 across all four model families.
  \item \textbf{Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}: To fit multi-billion-parameter backbones into 24~GB of memory without out-of-memory crashes, three of the four architectures relied on Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).
  \item \textbf{Preset Step Budgets}: Models were trained for fixed step budgets ranging between 30,000 and 100,000 steps (approximately 2.0 to 2.5 hours of wall-clock time per run), rather than training to verified loss convergence or using validation-driven checkpoint selection.
\end{itemize}

The four adapted architectures represent distinct structural paradigms in generalist robot learning:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo} (93M parameters): A transformer-based diffusion policy combining visual tokenizers, language conditioning, and a continuous diffusion action head. Octo represents the compact baseline; it was fully fine-tuned for 100,000 steps at batch size 8 in approximately two hours, utilizing two history observation frames.
  \item \textbf{SmolVLA} (450M parameters): A lightweight autoregressive VLA architecture. Adapted for 100,000 steps at batch size 8 using LoRA with rank $r = 64$ and $\alpha = 64$ applied to the query and value projections of the language expert and the state/action linear projections. Training employed a cosine learning rate decay from $1\times 10^{-3}$ to $1\times 10^{-4}$ over two wall-clock hours.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_0}$} (3.3B parameters): A flow-matching vision--language--action model built on a PaliGemma backbone coupled with a specialized action expert. Adapted for 30,000 steps at batch size 8 using LoRA with rank $r_{\text{vlm}} = 16$ on the vision--language trunk and $r_{\text{act}} = 32$ on the action expert, targeting Gemma attention and feed-forward layers. Optimization used AdamW at $2.5\times 10^{-5}$ with a 1,000-step linear warmup and cosine decay to $2.5\times 10^{-6}$ over 2.5 hours. Exponential moving average (EMA) weights were disabled.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$}: An expanded architectural revision of the $\pi_0$ family, fine-tuned under an identical 30,000-step budget at batch size 8 with $r_{\text{vlm}} = 16$ and $r_{\text{act}} = 32$. Training utilized a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-5}$ with a 10,000-step warmup held constant thereafter.
\end{enumerate}

All four policies operate with an action chunk horizon of 50 steps. Models were trained strictly on atomic demonstrations; no composite task demonstrations were provided during adaptation. Consequently, evaluating composite tasks tests whether policies can sustain multi-stage execution when guided solely by sequential oracle prompts.

\section{Evaluation scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

To evaluate model robustness systematically, we structured our experimental protocol into a four-tier evaluation ladder. Rather than testing only nominal performance or conflating all environmental variations into a single randomized test, each tier introduces an isolated, well-defined dimension of distribution shift:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Roman*.},leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Seeds} (Exact In-Domain Reproduction): Re-executes the exact procedural configurations, fixture placements, and product coordinates used during demonstration collection. This isolates pure imitation fidelity from any environmental shift.
  \item \textbf{Pose} (In-Domain Spatial Perturbation): Preserves the training store plans, textures, and product identities, but perturbs the robot's initial base placement in front of the fixture ($\pm 25$~cm translation, $\pm 22.5^{\circ}$ yaw). This tests whether the policy has memorized fixed open-loop trajectories or can adjust to local base positioning offsets.
  \item \textbf{Layout} (Visual and Geometric Shift): Introduces completely unseen store floor plans alongside novel wall, floor, and ceiling texture sets. The target tasks and products remain familiar, but the surrounding visual clutter, aisle geometry, and lighting perspectives are entirely new.
  \item \textbf{Pairing} (Compositional Skill Transfer): Evaluates zero-shot task--item composition. The policy is instructed to manipulate a target product that was present in the demonstration corpus for \emph{other} tasks, but was never demonstrated for the evaluated task. This tests whether the model can bind a known visual object to an independently learned manipulation primitive.
\end{enumerate}

Two important methodological caveats emerged during our post-evaluation protocol audit. First, in the door-manipulation tasks (Open and Close), the nominal Layout condition did not sample disjoint floor plans; it re-instantiated the training store layouts, varying only surface textures and initial base poses. Second, in the Pairing condition, while target products were withheld from task demonstrations, those items remained physically present on the shelves as background clutter during training scenes. Thus, Pairing tests compositional instruction binding rather than perceptual zero-shot recognition of an unseen physical object.

Each task--item--fixture triplet is evaluated over 100 Monte Carlo rollouts. Under Pose and Layout, Basket and Board pool three triplets (300 trials per cell), while Floor, Open, and Close pool two triplets (200 trials). In the Pairing condition, door tasks are marked not applicable (--) because their instructions manipulate fixed fixture handles rather than product entities. Composite tasks evaluate sequential execution across 100 trials; because partial stage logs were discarded during aggregation, composite scores record full-chain success.

\section{Success and side-effect checks}

Evaluating retail manipulation requires monitoring the entire physical scene rather than checking whether a target coordinate changed. Knocking over adjacent cereal boxes to reach a jar of jam constitutes a severe commercial failure.

The evaluation engine enforces the strict composite predicate formulated in Equation~\ref{eq:predicate-success}:
\begin{equation}
  y = \mathbb{I}[o^* \in G]\;\mathbb{I}[\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\text{robot}}\rVert_2 \leq v_{\text{tol}}]\;\prod_{o \in \mathcal{N}(o^*)} \mathbb{I}[\lVert\Delta \mathbf{p}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_p \;\land\; \lVert\Delta \mathbf{q}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_q].
  \label{eq:strict-success}
\end{equation}
The target item $o^*$ must settle within destination volume $G$, the robot platform must settle below velocity threshold $v_{\text{tol}} = 0.2$~m/s, and collateral objects $o \in \mathcal{N}(o^*)$ must not exceed translational ($\epsilon_p = 0.1$~m) or rotational ($\epsilon_q = 0.1$) displacement tolerances.

Our audit surfaced the exact geometric boundaries implemented in code:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Basket Destination}: Evaluated as an axis-aligned bounding cube of $0.4\times 0.4\times 0.4$~meters anchored to the robot's base coordinates (expanded to $0.5$~m for Honey Stars). The collateral check $\mathcal{N}(o^*)$ monitors only items residing on the target fixture shelf, ignoring collisions with distant fixtures during base navigation.
  \item \textbf{Board Transfer Destination}: Enforces a tight horizontal window ($\pm 0.1$~m) centered $0.397$~meters vertically above the item's initial pose, with scene-wide collateral monitoring.
  \item \textbf{Door Articulation}: Showcase doors must reach $1.57 \pm 0.2$~rad (open) or $0 \pm 0.2$~rad (close); refrigerator covers require $0.624 \pm 0.1$~rad.
\end{itemize}

Mean success rates are reported as percentages. To quantify transfer degradation between in-domain perturbations and environmental shifts, we compute absolute percentage-point differences:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S_{m,t} = S_{m,t,\mathrm{Pose}} - S_{m,t,\mathrm{Layout}}.
  \label{eq:degradation}
\end{equation}
Absolute point deltas are mathematically essential: when baseline performance hovers near zero, relative percentage ratios (e.g., $S_{\text{Layout}} / S_{\text{Pose}}$) become unstable, divide by zero, or exaggerate single-episode differences.

\section{Failure annotation}

To uncover the physical mechanisms behind unsuccessful rollouts, we developed an 11-stage chronological failure coding taxonomy. Across the four model families and four evaluation scenarios, unsuccessful episodes were sampled for fine-grained inspection, yielding an archived diagnostic dataset of 2,906 annotated failure trajectories.

The taxonomy tracks the temporal progression of a manipulation episode, assigning the failure to the earliest primary breakdown observed:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.15em]
  \item \textbf{Task}: The robot initiates an action sequence corresponding to an entirely different operational task.
  \item \textbf{Target}: The robot approaches, aligns with, or attempts to manipulate an incorrect product entity on the shelf.
  \item \textbf{Move}: Gross mobile-base navigation fails to approach the vicinity of the designated fixture.
  \item \textbf{Pre-grasp}: The end-effector reaches the fixture but misaligns spatially or angularly relative to the target item.
  \item \textbf{Grasp}: The gripper fingers close on the target object but fail to establish a stable friction hold, slipping off or missing contact.
  \item \textbf{Drop}: The object is initially lifted but slips or is dropped during transport.
  \item \textbf{Displace}: The manipulator or target object collides with and displaces neighboring stock beyond permissible tolerances.
  \item \textbf{Pre-place}: Transport brings the object near the destination, but the arm fails to position the item over the receptacle.
  \item \textbf{Place-coord}: The item is released outside the acceptable geometric bounding envelope of the destination.
  \item \textbf{Place}: The item tumbles, bounces out, or destabilizes upon release.
  \item \textbf{Partial}: Articulated door manipulation initiates but stalls prior to satisfying the required angular threshold.
\end{enumerate}

This taxonomy provides high diagnostic utility, but its methodological limits must be acknowledged. First, because labels are assigned using an earliest-primary criterion, subsequent errors occurring downstream of the initial fault are masked. Second, annotations were conducted by a single researcher without multi-annotator cross-validation. Consequently, failure proportions should be interpreted as revealing macroscopic mechanical bottlenecks rather than high-precision point probabilities.

\section{Validity considerations}
\label{sec:validity}

Evaluating robotic policies in simulation involves critical validity boundaries:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Construct Validity}: While the generator synthesizes large-scale $24\times 15$-meter supermarkets, policy evaluation does not exercise whole-store navigation. Every episode begins with the robot positioned 1.4~meters in front of the active fixture, facing it. Aisle geometry and surrounding shelving provide visual background and local approach constraints, but policies are never required to traverse store-scale routes. Furthermore, non-rigid deformable packaging, barcode scanning, barcode orientation, and dynamic human obstacles are excluded from the current simulation.
  \item \textbf{Internal Validity}: The four baseline models reflect fixed-step adaptation recipes constrained by consumer hardware, rather than identical convergence points. Furthermore, two operational constraints dominate the control loop: (1) policies execute open-loop action chunks of 50 simulation steps between visual observations, blinding them to transient slip or contact events, and (2) the prismatic torso joint is clamped to zero throughout, permanently eliminating vertical whole-body reach.
  \item \textbf{External Validity}: Rigid-body simulation in ManiSkill3 does not capture sensor noise, tactile deformation, packaging compliance, or motor backlash inherent to physical hardware. The benchmark evaluates algorithmic competence under simulated Newtonian dynamics, serving as an inspectable filter prior to physical deployment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol traceability and quality control}
\label{sec:traceability}

A foundational objective of this dissertation is establishing rigorous protocol hygiene. In complex simulation studies, empirical results frequently become uninterpretable because seed definitions, checkpoint weights, or sensor normalizations are lost across iterations.

Achieving complete traceability requires an unbroken evidentiary chain linking every cell in a results table back to its specific model checkpoint, commit hash, scene JSON, camera buffer calibration, and step-level rollout log. While our archived results retain comprehensive cell summaries and verified transformation scripts, raw per-step trajectory files were not fully preserved. Future iterations of this benchmark must mandate automated logging of per-step simulator states, machine-readable failure annotations, and automated unit tests for success predicates. These protocols ensure that future evaluations remain fully auditable, reproducible, and extensible across the broader research community.

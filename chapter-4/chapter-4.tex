\section{Experimental logic and reproduction philosophy}

A benchmark is only as informative as its ability to explain why an embodied system succeeds or breaks down. In robotics, that diagnostic link is notoriously fragile. A policy might fail because its visual encoder misinterprets a branded logo under novel lighting, because its motion planner clips the edge of a shelf, or simply because its low-level controller cannot track an aggressive trajectory. If the evaluation protocol aggregates these disparate failure modes into a single scalar success percentage, it conceals the very physical insights it was built to expose.

This dissertation approaches benchmark reproduction not as a mechanical re-execution of published commands, but as an independent empirical investigation. Replicating an embodied manipulation testbed forces an encounter with reality: code repositories contain unstated assumptions, socket interfaces behave differently under load, and local computational budgets dictate how many rollouts can be stepped before deadlines expire. 

Our experimental methodology bridges the three core research questions to concrete, observable physical metrics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Simulation Feasibility and Usability)}: Verifying that procedural store plans are not just topologically diverse, but geometrically navigable, physically collision-free, and computationally stable when stepping complex scenes in ManiSkill3~\cite{taomaniskill3}.
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Cross-Architecture Inference and Concurrency)}: Designing and validating a standardized multi-view inference client capable of serving structurally divergent vision--language--action models under identical observation buffers, while auditing the operational characteristics of their server deployments.
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (Empirical Competence and Control Bottlenecks)}: Deploying released, authoritative model checkpoints across a disciplined 42-configuration evaluation ladder spanning four distribution-shift tiers, recording 2,520 fully verified rollouts, and conducting a forensic autopsy of physical failure.
\end{itemize}

The fundamental experimental unit is an individual policy rollout. Each episode is governed by five explicit coordinates: the target manipulation task, the active product identity, the destination fixture, the procedural environment seed, and the designated distribution tier. The policy operates strictly as a sensorimotor controller: it consumes natural-language prompts, synchronized RGB images, and proprioceptive joint buffers. All privileged simulator state---exact mesh bounding boxes, contact normals, and joint velocities---remains encapsulated within ManiSkill3 for deterministic scene resetting and predicate verification.

\section{Simulation deployment and runtime interfacing}

Deploying the RoboBenchMart simulation environment locally required synthesizing multiple decoupled software subsystems into a coherent evaluation pipeline. 

The physical substrate runs entirely in ManiSkill3, leveraging GPU-accelerated PhysX rigid-body dynamics and multi-camera batch rasterization. Scenes are instantiated from pre-generated procedural layout packages (\texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_demo\_envs}, git revision \texttt{3dabfaaf9bdf2ba886264a1160dab68ac7043d00}), comprising 466 scene files totaling approximately 87.6~megabytes. Loading pre-synthesized JSON scene descriptions rather than generating store geometry on-the-fly inside the reset loop ensures that every rollout executes against frozen, reproducible geometry.

The Fetch mobile manipulator interfaces with the simulator via a standardized sensory schema:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Bilateral Shoulder Views}: Dual RGB cameras mounted at the left and right shoulders ($256\times 256\times 3$), providing wide-angle stereoscopic visual coverage of the fixture workspace and surrounding shelf clutter.
  \item \textbf{In-Hand Wrist View}: An eye-in-hand RGB camera ($128\times 128\times 3$) rigidly fixed to the gripper mount, capturing high-resolution local perspective during contact acquisition.
  \item \textbf{Proprioceptive State}: Continuous joint positions and velocities across the 7-DOF arm, parallel gripper fingers, and mobile chassis.
  \item \textbf{Task Prompt}: A natural-language string designating the active product and target fixture (e.g., \texttt{"pick the green tea box from the second shelf and place it in the basket"}).
\end{itemize}

Policies command the robot through a 13-dimensional continuous action vector:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}_t = \left[\mathbf{q}^{\text{arm}}_t,\ a^{\text{grip}}_t,\ \mathbf{q}^{\text{body}}_t,\ v^{\text{base}}_t,\ \omega^{\text{base}}_t\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{13},
  \label{eq:action-vector-method}
\end{equation}
comprising seven arm joint target angles $\mathbf{q}^{\text{arm}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^7$, a scalar gripper position command $a^{\text{grip}}_t \in [0, 1]$, three body joint targets $\mathbf{q}^{\text{body}}_t = [q_{\text{torso}}, q_{\text{pan}}, q_{\text{tilt}}] \in \mathbb{R}^3$, and planar base linear and angular velocities $(v^{\text{base}}_t, \omega^{\text{base}}_t)$. The simulation client communicates with policy inference backends across local network sockets using remote procedure calls (RPC).

\section{Compute allocation and inference concurrency}
\label{sec:compute}

Conducting a rigorous 2,520-rollout empirical evaluation under academic hardware limits requires disciplined resource engineering. Rather than attempting to train or fine-tune multi-billion-parameter models locally---which under constrained VRAM would force compromises on batch size, step budgets, and convergence---this study evaluates the authoritative, publicly released fine-tuned checkpoints provided by the benchmark authors. This design isolates physical execution from local training noise.

We evaluate two architecturally divergent vision--language--action foundation models:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo} (93M parameters): A transformer-based diffusion policy combining visual tokenizers, language conditioning, and a continuous diffusion action head. We evaluate checkpoint \texttt{1000000} from \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_octo} (revision \texttt{f3c8de450fc08fb5737d32442a2b1e6ab2993244}), served via \texttt{scripts/octo\_server.py}. The policy processes current observations with an observation history window of one frame ($H_{\text{obs}} = 1$), outputting action chunks of 50 steps.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$}: A large-scale vision--language--action model pairing a PaliGemma vision--language backbone with a specialized continuous flow-matching action expert. We evaluate released weights from \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_pi05} (revision \texttt{533ad107347c3568dc2456d1586b665889f0ed18}), deployed via the OpenPI server architecture under configuration \texttt{pi05\_eval\_rbm}. Action chunks are predicted over 50 steps.
\end{enumerate}

Physical GPU memory constraints directly shaped how each model was served during execution:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo Execution Architecture}: Because Octo is relatively lightweight (93M parameters), multiple model instances could reside in GPU memory simultaneously. Execution proceeded under batch identifier \texttt{octo\_parallel\_20260928\_021558}. To verify socket stability and baseline protocol adherence, the first 17 configurations were executed strictly serially. Once stability was confirmed, the remaining 25 configurations were parallelized across four independent Octo server instances running on separate ports, substantially reducing total evaluation wall-clock time.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$ Execution Architecture}: Due to the substantial VRAM footprint of the PaliGemma backbone and flow-matching network, launching four distinct OpenPI model instances would trigger GPU out-of-memory faults. Execution was therefore organized under batch identifier \texttt{pi05\_parallel\_20261002\_105333} using a shared-service architecture: a single OpenPI server process loaded on port 8000, while four independent ManiSkill3 simulation clients executed concurrently, dispatching asynchronous RPC inference requests to the shared server.
\end{itemize}

This runtime distinction has important methodological ramifications. Because Octo utilized independent server instances while $\pi_{0.5}$ serialized requests through a shared queue, pseudorandom number consumption sequences and asynchronous response latencies differed between the two runs. Consequently, rollouts between Octo and $\pi_{0.5}$ cannot be treated as strictly paired, step-by-step counterfactuals. Instead, they represent standardized benchmark assessments of each model's independent operational capability across identical task configurations.

\section{The 42-configuration evaluation matrix}
\label{sec:matrix}

To measure policy generalization systematically, we evaluate both models across a matrix of 42 atomic task configurations defined in \texttt{bash/eval\_model.sh}. The matrix organizes tasks into four progressive distribution-shift tiers:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Roman*.},leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Seeds} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_train} suffix): Re-executes the nominal store plans, fixture placements, and product arrangements used during demonstration collection with zero base offset. This tier evaluates in-domain imitation fidelity under nominal initial conditions.
  \item \textbf{Pose} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_robo} suffix): Preserves training store layouts, textures, and product identities, but perturbs the robot's initial chassis placement in front of the fixture ($\pm 25$~cm translation, $\pm 22.5^{\circ}$ yaw; seed 10000). This isolates the policy's capacity to compensate for local base positioning errors.
  \item \textbf{Layout} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_uns} suffix; seed 42000): Introduces novel procedural store floor plans alongside unseen wall, floor, and ceiling textures. Target tasks and products remain familiar, but surrounding visual clutter and perspective geometries shift.
  \item \textbf{Pairing} (6 configurations; \texttt{\_ood} suffix; seed 42000): Evaluates zero-shot task--item composition. The model is commanded to manipulate a target product that was present in training demonstrations for other tasks, but was never demonstrated for the evaluated task (e.g., placing a Nivea cream box on a shelf board, or picking a Slam juice into a cart).
\end{enumerate}

Two vital methodological caveats govern this matrix. First, door manipulation tasks (Open and Close) have no meaningful Pairing condition because their instructions address fixed fixture handles rather than product entities; these cells are marked not applicable (--). Second, our protocol audit revealed that in door tasks, the nominal Layout condition does not instantiate disjoint store plans; it reuses the training floor layouts, varying only textures and starting poses. Consequently, door Layout tests texture invariance rather than geometric floor-plan transfer.

Each configuration is evaluated over 30 independent Monte Carlo rollouts. Why 30 rollouts? In robot evaluation, single-trial runs yield noisy, anecdotal metrics, while 100-rollout sweeps across 42 configurations (8,400 episodes across two models) would exceed available GPU compute budgets by a factor of three. Evaluating 30 rollouts per configuration strikes the optimal empirical balance: it amasses 1,260 rollouts per model (2,520 episodes in aggregate), providing sufficient statistical resolution to detect meaningful performance trends while remaining computationally tractable on local infrastructure.

Performance is aggregated across five canonical atomic task families:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Basket} (Pick to Basket; 3 items: Fanta, Nivea, Honey Stars): Pools 3 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (90 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Nestl{\'e}, Slam).
  \item \textbf{Board} (Board to Board; 3 items: Duff, Nestl{\'e}, Vanish): Pools 3 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (90 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Nivea, Fanta).
  \item \textbf{Floor} (Pick from Floor; 2 items: Heinz Beans, Slam): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Fanta, Duff).
  \item \textbf{Open} (Fridge and Showcase Open): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each).
  \item \textbf{Close} (Fridge and Showcase Close): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each).
\end{itemize}

This yields 360 episodes under Seeds, 360 under Pose, 360 under Layout, and 180 under Pairing per model, totaling exactly 1,260 evaluated episodes per model (2,520 rollouts overall).

Episode horizons enforce explicit simulation step budgets: Board and Floor tasks allow a maximum of 750 simulator steps; Basket picking allows 600 steps; Refrigerator tasks allow 500 steps; and Showcase tasks allow 1,000 steps.

\section{Control constraints and task success predicates}

Evaluating retail manipulation requires strict monitoring of whole-scene integrity. Knocking over adjacent stock while reaching for a target item constitutes an operational failure.

The evaluation engine enforces the composite predicate:
\begin{equation}
  y = \mathbb{I}[o^* \in G]\;\mathbb{I}[\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\text{robot}}\rVert_2 \leq v_{\text{tol}}]\;\prod_{o \in \mathcal{N}(o^*)} \mathbb{I}[\lVert\Delta \mathbf{p}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_p \;\land\; \lVert\Delta \mathbf{q}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_q].
  \label{eq:strict-success-method}
\end{equation}
The target item $o^*$ must settle within the designated target volume $G$, the robot platform must come to rest ($\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\text{robot}}\rVert_2 \leq 0.2$~m/s), and surrounding collateral objects $\mathcal{N}(o^*)$ must remain undisturbed within translational ($\epsilon_p = 0.1$~m) and rotational ($\epsilon_q = 0.1$) tolerances.

Our inspection confirmed the exact geometric parameters implemented in code:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Basket Destination}: Evaluated as an axis-aligned bounding cube of $0.4\times 0.4\times 0.4$~meters anchored to the robot's base coordinates (expanded to $0.5$~m for large cereal packaging such as Honey Stars). Collateral checking monitors only items residing on the target fixture shelf.
  \item \textbf{Board Transfer Destination}: Enforces a horizontal window of $\pm 0.1$~m centered $0.397$~meters vertically above the source shelf, with scene-wide collateral monitoring.
  \item \textbf{Door Articulation}: Showcase doors must reach $1.57 \pm 0.2$~rad (open) or $0 \pm 0.2$~rad (close); refrigerator covers require $0.624 \pm 0.1$~rad.
\end{itemize}

Beyond spatial tolerances, our code audit revealed two decisive physical constraints governing low-level control:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{50-Step Open-Loop Action Chunking}: Both models predict action chunks of 50 steps. The simulator executes all 50 steps open-loop before returning a fresh visual observation, blinding the policy to transient contact deflections during critical pre-grasp phases.
  \item \textbf{Rigid Spine Clamping ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$)}: In the evaluation client, low-level logic explicitly overwrites the torso-lift and head-pan command dimensions with zero at every simulation step. Because joint setpoints are interpreted as absolute coordinates, this forces the prismatic torso to remain permanently locked at its mechanical minimum ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$). Across every rollout, the robot operates with an entirely immobilized vertical spine.
\end{itemize}

\section{Artifact logging, data boundaries, and traceability}
\label{sec:traceability-method}

To ensure complete experimental auditability without relying on opaque aggregate numbers, all 2,520 rollouts were logged with verified artifact manifests.

Each of the 42 configurations generates an execution log containing an explicit terminal summary (\texttt{Results: [list of successful episodes]}). Every rollout produces a full MP4 video recording, indexed by configuration and episode number. In the project repository, \texttt{episode\_outcomes.csv} maps every rollout to its terminal binary outcome, while \texttt{artifact\_manifest.csv} and \texttt{log\_manifest.csv} document the SHA-256 checksums, byte sizes, and paths of all 2,688 generated output files (1,344 files per model, including 1,260 MP4 videos stored via Git LFS).

Our inspection identified a crucial data boundary: while video recordings and text logs are complete, container HDF5 files are exactly 800 bytes each, and JSON \texttt{episodes} arrays are unpopulated. Consequently, the archive does not preserve continuous per-step joint trajectories. Episode success indices are derived directly from verified runtime environment logs.

\section{Validity considerations}
\label{sec:validity-method}

Evaluating robotic policies in simulation involves critical validity boundaries:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Construct Validity}: While the generator synthesizes large-scale $24\times 15$-meter supermarkets, policy evaluation does not exercise whole-store navigation. Every episode begins with the robot positioned 1.4~meters in front of the active fixture, facing it. Aisle geometry and surrounding shelving provide visual background and local approach constraints, but policies are never required to traverse store-scale routes. Non-rigid packaging and human obstacles are excluded.
  \item \textbf{Internal Validity}: The evaluated checkpoints represent public fine-tuned weights rather than locally trained models. Furthermore, two operational constraints dominate the control loop: (1) policies execute open-loop action chunks of 50 simulation steps between visual observations, blinding them to transient contact events, and (2) client logic explicitly overwrites the torso-lift and head-pan action elements with zero at every timestep ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$), permanently locking the robot into an immobilized vertical spine.
  \item \textbf{External Validity}: Rigid-body simulation in ManiSkill3 does not capture sensor noise, tactile compliance, packaging deformation, or motor backlash inherent to physical hardware.
\end{itemize}

By documenting these constraints candidly, this methodology establishes a grounded, transparent framework for analyzing the empirical findings in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

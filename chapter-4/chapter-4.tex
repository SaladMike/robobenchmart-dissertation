\section{What was measured}
\label{sec:study-design}

The study is an independent re-evaluation. I took two policy checkpoints that the RoboBenchMart authors released after fine-tuning them on their demonstration data, served them without further training, and ran them through the benchmark's 42 atomic evaluation configurations using the public code and scenes. No model was trained or fine-tuned on my side. No composite task was run, and no failure was annotated.

The unit of observation is one episode: a reset of one configuration with one seed, followed by policy-driven control until the episode terminates or reaches its step cap. Its outcome is a single binary flag, the environment's success predicate at the end of the episode. Each configuration was run for 30 episodes per model. With 42 configurations this gives 1,260 episodes per model, and since the two models were run on the same seeds, 2,520 episodes that can be matched by seed.

The policy is treated as a black box behind a network interface. It receives three RGB images, the robot's proprioceptive state and a language instruction, and returns a chunk of actions. Everything else, including object poses, contact information and the predicate itself, stays inside the simulator.

\section{Checkpoints and versions}
\label{sec:versions}

Both checkpoints were downloaded from the upstream Hugging Face organisation and pinned by commit (\ref{tab:revisions} in Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The Octo checkpoint, \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_octo} at \texttt{f3c8de45}, was published on 4~November 2025; its weights sit in a directory named \texttt{1000000}. The $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint, \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_pi05} at \texttt{533ad107}, followed a day later.

How they were trained is reported upstream, not observed by me. According to the RoboBenchMart paper, both were fully fine-tuned on the benchmark's 2,976 motion-planned demonstrations: Octo for one million steps at batch size 128 on eight A100 GPUs, and $\pi_{0.5}$ for 500,000 steps at batch size 256, also on eight A100s~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. The directory name of the Octo checkpoint is consistent with the reported step count. I have no further information about either training run.

The code and scenes I evaluated on are later than the checkpoints. The benchmark code is upstream commit \texttt{33ca547} of 13~July 2026 plus the repairs described in Section~\ref{sec:execution-plan}. The scenes are the \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_demo\_envs} release \texttt{3dabfaaf} of 22~January 2026, whose commit message reads ``Upload new MP data with regen layouts''. Between November 2025 and July 2026 the upstream repository changed the initial joint configuration of the robot, the lighting, parts of the motion planner and robot model, and regenerated the demonstration data. The evaluated configuration is therefore not the one the checkpoints were trained against. I did not try to reconstruct that earlier configuration; doing so would have been a different experiment. The mismatch is documented here because it bears directly on how the results can be read (Section~\ref{sec:upstream-gap}).

\section{Serving the policies and the control loop}
\label{sec:control}

Each model is served by a websocket server, and the evaluation client, \texttt{scripts/eval\_policy\_client.py}, runs the simulator and talks to the server.

The Octo server (\texttt{scripts/octo\_server.py}) builds a policy with one frame of observation history, a prediction horizon of 50 and an execution horizon of 50, so each request returns 50 actions. One history frame matches the upstream repository's README, which describes the checkpoint as Octo-base ``finetuned with 1 history image and 50 action horizon''. The upstream paper gives two frames; I followed the released server. The $\pi_{0.5}$ policy is served by OpenPI at revision \texttt{c23745b5} with a RoboBenchMart adapter (\texttt{scripts/add\_rbm.patch}) and the policy configuration \texttt{pi05\_eval\_rbm}. The adapter returns the first 13 dimensions of the model's action output. It does not set the action horizon, so the chunk length is whatever OpenPI's default model configuration specifies; in the public OpenPI source this default is 50, but I did not confirm it on the revision used, and the chunk length for $\pi_{0.5}$ should be treated as unverified.

The client loop is short, and its details matter. For each episode it resets the environment with the episode seed, reads the instruction, and then repeats three steps: send the current observation, receive a chunk, and execute every action in the chunk before asking for the next one. The chunk is executed open loop. Before each action is applied, the client overwrites elements 8 and 9 of the 13-dimensional action with zero. Termination and the step cap are checked only after a complete chunk. The recorded outcome is the success flag returned by the last executed step.

Which joints elements 8 and 9 address follows from the controller definition rather than from anything in the client. The RoboBenchMart robot class \texttt{DSFetchBasket} extends ManiSkill's Fetch agent, whose \texttt{pd\_joint\_pos} controller concatenates the arm (seven joints), the gripper (one), the body group and the base (two velocities); the body group is ordered head pan, head tilt, torso lift. Indices 8 and 9 are therefore head pan and head tilt, and the torso lift is index 10, which the client leaves alone. \texttt{DSFetchBasket} replaces the body controller with an absolute position controller (\texttt{use\_delta=False}), so the overwrite holds the head at its central position. This mapping is inferred from the code; I did not print the controller's joint list during the runs. Earlier drafts of this work described the torso as clamped to zero. That description was wrong, and Section~\ref{sec:protocol-properties} returns to it.

\section{Configurations and conditions}
\label{sec:conditions-def}

The 42 configurations are those of \texttt{bash/eval\_model.sh}. They cover the five atomic task families defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:system}: Board (move an item between shelf boards; Duff, Nestl\'e and Vanish), Floor (pick an item from the floor; Heinz beans and SLAM), Basket (pick an item into the robot's basket; Fanta, Nivea and Honey Stars), Open and Close (a fridge lid and a showcase door). Each task--item--fixture combination appears under four conditions, which the script calls \texttt{train}, \texttt{robo}, \texttt{uns} and \texttt{ood}. I use the names below; the upstream paper uses ``Train Seeds'', ``In-Domain'', ``Unseen Scenes'' and ``Unseen Scenes \& Items''.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.25em]
\item[Seeds (12 configurations)] Training scenes. The episode seeds are the first 30 seeds listed in the demonstration file for that combination (for example 0, 4, 8, \ldots). The robot's starting pose is drawn from the episode random stream, so in principle each episode starts where the corresponding demonstration did. In practice this holds only if the environment code matches the code that generated the demonstrations, which after the 2026 changes it need not.
\item[Pose (12)] The same scenes and episode seeds as Seeds, but the starting pose is drawn from a separate random stream seeded 10,000, 10,001, and so on. Everything except the robot's start is unchanged.
\item[Layout (12)] For Board and Floor, separate scene directories (\texttt{test\_unseen\_scenes\_*}); for Basket, the directory \texttt{test\_unseen\_items\_pick\_to\_basket}. Episode seeds run from 42,000 and pose seeds from 10,000. For the four door configurations the scene directories are the training directories; only the seeds change.
\item[Pairing (6)] A product paired with a task for which it was not demonstrated: Board with Nivea and Fanta, Floor with Fanta and Duff, Basket with Nestl\'e and SLAM. Seeds as in Layout. Door tasks have no item and therefore no Pairing configuration.
\end{description}

The starting-pose perturbation is the same mechanism under all conditions; what changes is the random stream it draws from. Before perturbation the base is placed 1.4~m in front of the target fixture, facing it. For Basket, Board and Floor the base is then moved away from the fixture by up to 0.2~m, sideways by up to $\pm0.25$~m, and rotated by up to $\pm22.5^{\circ}$. For the showcase it is first moved back a further 0.15~m and then perturbed by up to 0.62~m away, $\pm0.62$~m sideways and $\pm22.5^{\circ}$. For the fridge it is moved \emph{towards} the fixture by up to 0.2~m, with the same sideways and yaw ranges as the pick tasks. All draws are uniform.

\section{Success predicates}

The success flag is computed by the environment's \texttt{evaluate} method, which Chapter~\ref{chap:system} describes. I did not change any predicate. Two points are worth repeating here because they shape the results. Every predicate requires the robot to be nearly static, so an episode in which the item lands in the right place while the base is still moving does not count until the robot settles. And the door predicates differ between fixtures: the fridge lid must be within 0.1~rad of its target angle, a showcase door within 0.2~rad.

\section{Execution}
\label{sec:execution-plan}

For Octo, I first ran the 42 configurations as a single serial batch against one server, in the order of the script. That batch failed at the first floor task and had to be resumed twice before the remaining 25 configurations were moved to four independent servers. For $\pi_{0.5}$, I distributed the configurations from the start across four client processes sharing one OpenPI server. Section~\ref{sec:execution} gives the details. Three code changes were needed for the protocol to run at all: restoring an import in the floor environment, reading the instruction from the unwrapped environment, and giving each Octo connection its own policy instance. Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists these and the smaller changes to timeouts and recording.

Because of the mixed execution, the two models' episodes are matched by seed but are not guaranteed to be identical simulations. The Octo episodes were produced by different server processes for different configurations, the $\pi_{0.5}$ episodes by one shared server, and the runs took place on different days. I refer to the two sets as seed-matched and avoid calling them paired.

\section{Recording outcomes}
\label{sec:recording}

For each episode the client writes a video, and at the end of each configuration it prints a \texttt{Results:} line listing the seeds of the successful episodes and the success rate. The progress bar also reports running mean and maximum episode length. These two kinds of line are the primary record. I mapped each seed onto its video file, which gives a per-episode table of 1,260 rows per model, and recorded the SHA-256 hash of every log, video and container file.

The trajectory containers that ManiSkill's recording wrapper writes (an HDF5 file and a JSON file per configuration) are effectively empty. The evaluation script does not pass \texttt{--save-traj}, and the client calls \texttt{flush\_trajectory()} only when it is given. So the saved evidence is an outcome per episode, not a sequence of states and actions. I mention this because the container files exist and could be mistaken for trajectory data.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

The main quantity is the success count $k$ out of $n$ episodes for a model $m$, task family $t$ and condition $c$, and the corresponding rate
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{m,t,c} = \frac{k_{m,t,c}}{n_{t,c}}, \qquad
  k_{m,t,c} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{t,c}} y_i ,
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the success flag of episode $i$. Counts are pooled within a family, never across families: a model's door results and pick-and-place results are reported separately, and I give no overall score.

With $n$ between 30 and 180 and many counts at or near zero, the normal-approximation interval is unusable. I report the Wilson score interval~\cite{wilson1927probable}, which behaves well for small samples and proportions near zero~\cite{brown2001interval}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}}, \qquad z = 1.96,
  \label{eq:wilson}
\end{equation}
which remains informative at $k=0$, where its upper bound is $z^2/(n+z^2)$.

Comparisons between two counts use the two-sided Fisher exact test. For the comparison between models I also report a sign test on the seeds where exactly one model succeeded. All tests are descriptive. There is no pre-registered hypothesis, the comparisons were chosen after seeing the data, and I make no correction for multiplicity; a $p$-value here indicates how surprising a difference would be under sampling variation alone, nothing more. Differences between conditions are stated as counts (15/60 against 10/60) rather than as relative degradation, because ratios of small rates are unstable.

\section{Validity}
\label{sec:validity}

The internal validity of the comparison between conditions is limited mainly by sample size. Thirty episodes per configuration give intervals of roughly $\pm15$ percentage points around a rate of 25\%, and several of the comparisons the protocol was designed for cannot be resolved at that width.

The construct validity of the conditions depends on details of the scene directories. Door Layout reuses the training scenes and therefore does not test new geometry. Pairing can only show a drop if the corresponding Seeds or Pose cell is above zero. Every episode starts within about 2~m of its fixture, so none of the conditions tests navigation through the store.

The external validity question in this study is unusually concrete. The results describe two specific checkpoints evaluated on a specific, later version of the benchmark, served by specific software. They are evidence about whether the released artefacts reproduce the upstream results today. They are not evidence about what Octo or $\pi_{0.5}$ can learn from these demonstrations, and they are not evidence about physical robots.

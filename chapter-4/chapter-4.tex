\section{What was measured}
\label{sec:study-design}

This is a re-evaluation, not a training study. The RoboBenchMart authors fine-tuned policies on their demonstrations and published the checkpoints; I downloaded two of them, served them as they were, and ran them through the benchmark's 42 atomic evaluation configurations on the public code and scenes. I trained nothing. I ran no composite tasks and annotated no failures.

Everything is counted in episodes. An episode starts with a reset of one configuration under one seed; the policy then drives the robot until the environment signals termination or the step cap is reached. What comes out is one bit, the environment's success flag at the end. I ran 30 episodes per configuration and model, which across 42 configurations makes 1,260 per model. Because both models saw the same seeds, the 2,520 episodes can be lined up seed by seed.

From the policy's side the world is small. Three RGB images, the robot's joint positions and an instruction go in; a chunk of actions comes out. Object poses, contact forces and the success predicate never leave the simulator.

\section{Checkpoints and versions}
\label{sec:versions}

Both checkpoints come from the upstream Hugging Face organisation and are pinned by commit (\ref{tab:revisions} in Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The Octo checkpoint, \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_octo} at \texttt{f3c8de45}, appeared on 4~November 2025, its weights in a directory named \texttt{1000000}. The $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint, \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_pi05} at \texttt{533ad107}, followed the next day.

I know how they were trained only from the paper. According to it, both models were fully fine-tuned on the benchmark's 2,976 motion-planned demonstrations, Octo for one million steps at batch size 128 and $\pi_{0.5}$ for 500,000 steps at batch size 256, each on eight A100 GPUs~\cite{soshin2025robobenchmart}. The Octo directory name fits the reported step count. Beyond that I have no information about either run.

Here is the complication. The code and scenes I evaluated on are newer than the checkpoints, by months. The benchmark code is upstream commit \texttt{33ca547} of 13~July 2026, plus the repairs described in Section~\ref{sec:execution-plan}; the scenes are the \texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_demo\_envs} release \texttt{3dabfaaf} of 22~January 2026, committed as ``Upload new MP data with regen layouts''. In the months between, upstream changed the robot's initial joint configuration, the lighting, parts of the motion planner and the robot model, and regenerated the demonstration data. So the environment I ran is not the one the checkpoints were trained against. I chose not to reconstruct the older one, since that would have been a different experiment, but the mismatch shapes how every result in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} can be read (Section~\ref{sec:upstream-gap}).

\section{Serving the policies and the control loop}
\label{sec:control}

Each model sits behind a websocket server. The evaluation client, \texttt{scripts/eval\_policy\_client.py}, owns the simulator and queries the server.

For Octo, \texttt{scripts/octo\_server.py} builds a policy with one frame of observation history, a prediction horizon of 50 and an execution horizon of 50, so every request returns 50 actions. The single history frame agrees with the upstream README, which calls the checkpoint Octo-base ``finetuned with 1 history image and 50 action horizon''. The paper says two frames. I went with the released server. $\pi_{0.5}$ is served through OpenPI at revision \texttt{c23745b5}, with a RoboBenchMart adapter (\texttt{scripts/add\_rbm.patch}) and the policy configuration \texttt{pi05\_eval\_rbm}; the adapter keeps the first 13 dimensions of whatever the model outputs. It sets no action horizon of its own, so the chunk length is OpenPI's default. In the public OpenPI source that default is 50. I did not confirm it on the revision I ran, and for $\pi_{0.5}$ the chunk length should be read as unverified.

The client loop is only a few lines long, and almost every line affects the results. Reset with the episode seed. Read the instruction. Then, until the episode ends: send an observation, receive a chunk, execute all of it, and only then ask again. Nothing in between looks at the camera. Before each action reaches the environment, the client writes zeros into elements 8 and 9 of the 13-dimensional vector. Termination and the step cap are checked once per chunk, after its last action, and the outcome recorded for the episode is the success flag of that final step.

Which joints those two elements drive is decided by the controller, not the client. \texttt{DSFetchBasket}, RoboBenchMart's robot class, extends ManiSkill's Fetch agent, whose \texttt{pd\_joint\_pos} controller lays out its action as arm (seven joints), gripper (one), body group and base (two velocities), with the body group ordered head pan, head tilt, torso lift. Elements 8 and 9 are the head. The torso lift is element 10, and the client never touches it. Because \texttt{DSFetchBasket} switches the body group to absolute position targets (\texttt{use\_delta=False}), writing zero simply holds the head centred. I worked this mapping out from the code; I did not print the controller's joint list during the runs. Earlier drafts of this work said the torso was clamped to zero. I got that wrong, and Section~\ref{sec:protocol-properties} comes back to it.

\section{Configurations and conditions}
\label{sec:conditions-def}

The 42 configurations are exactly those in \texttt{bash/eval\_model.sh}. They span the five atomic families of Chapter~\ref{chap:system}: Board (move an item up a board; Duff, Nestl\'e, Vanish), Floor (return an item from the floor to the shelf; Heinz beans, SLAM), Basket (pick an item into the robot's basket; Fanta, Nivea, Honey Stars), and Open and Close (a fridge lid and a showcase door). Every task--item--fixture combination appears under four conditions, which the script labels \texttt{train}, \texttt{robo}, \texttt{uns} and \texttt{ood}. I call them by the names below. The upstream paper calls them ``Train Seeds'', ``In-Domain'', ``Unseen Scenes'' and ``Unseen Scenes \& Items''.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.25em]
\item[Seeds (12 configurations)] Training scenes. The episode seeds are the first 30 listed in the demonstration file for that combination. They are all multiples of four, with gaps: the fridge files begin 0, 4, 8, 12, while the Duff board file begins 8, 20, 24, 32. The starting pose is drawn from the episode's own random stream, so each episode should begin where its demonstration did, provided the environment code still matches the code that produced the demonstrations. After the 2026 changes, it need not.
\item[Pose (12)] The same scenes and episode seeds as Seeds. Only the starting pose changes, drawn from a separate stream seeded 10,000, 10,001, and so on.
\item[Layout (12)] Board and Floor load separate scene directories (\texttt{test\_unseen\_scenes\_*}); Basket loads \texttt{test\_unseen\_items\_pick\_to\_basket}. Episode seeds start at 42,000 and pose seeds at 10,000. The four door configurations load the training directories again, so for them only the seeds differ.
\item[Pairing (6)] A product paired with a task it was never demonstrated for: Board with Nivea and Fanta, Floor with Fanta and Duff, Basket with Nestl\'e and SLAM. Seeds as in Layout. Doors have no item, hence no Pairing.
\end{description}

The starting-pose perturbation works the same way under every condition; only its random stream differs. The base is first placed 1.4~m in front of the target fixture, facing it. For Basket, Board and Floor it is then pushed back by up to 0.2~m, sideways by up to $\pm0.25$~m, and turned by up to $\pm22.5^{\circ}$. The showcase variant steps back a further 0.15~m before a much wider perturbation, up to 0.62~m back, $\pm0.62$~m sideways and $\pm22.5^{\circ}$. The fridge is the odd one out: the base moves \emph{towards} it by up to 0.2~m, with the same sideways and yaw ranges as the pick tasks. Every draw is uniform.

\section{Success predicates}

Success is decided by each environment's \texttt{evaluate} method, described in Chapter~\ref{chap:system}, and I left every predicate as I found it. Two of their properties come up repeatedly in the results. The robot has to be almost motionless for an episode to count, so an item that lands in the right spot while the base is still rolling does not yet register. And the door tolerances differ by fixture: the fridge lid must come within 0.1~rad of its target, a showcase door within 0.2~rad.

\section{Execution}
\label{sec:execution-plan}

The plans for the two models diverged early. Octo began as a single serial batch, one server, configurations in script order. It broke at the first floor task, was resumed twice, and finished with the last 25 configurations spread over four independent servers. For $\pi_{0.5}$ I spread the configurations across four client processes from the start, all sharing one OpenPI server. Section~\ref{sec:execution} tells that story in more detail. Three code changes were unavoidable: restoring a missing import in the floor environment, reading the instruction from the unwrapped environment, and giving each Octo connection a policy of its own. Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists them, along with smaller changes to timeouts and recording.

The mixed execution has a consequence. Episodes from the two models share seeds, but nothing guarantees that they are identical simulations: Octo's came from several server processes, $\pi_{0.5}$'s from one shared server, on different days. I therefore call the two sets seed-matched and stop short of calling them paired.

\section{Recording outcomes}
\label{sec:recording}

The client writes a video for each episode. At the end of each configuration it prints a \texttt{Results:} line with the seeds of the successful episodes and the success rate, and throughout it updates a progress bar with running mean and maximum episode length. Those log lines are the primary record. I matched every seed to its video, giving a per-episode table of 1,260 rows for each model, and hashed every log, video and container file with SHA-256.

One trap is worth flagging. ManiSkill's recording wrapper also writes an HDF5 and a JSON file per configuration, and they look like trajectory archives. They are empty. The evaluation script does not pass \texttt{--save-traj}, and without it the client never calls \texttt{flush\_trajectory()}. What survives is one outcome per episode, not a sequence of states and actions.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

The basic quantity is a count: $k$ successes out of $n$ episodes for model $m$, task family $t$ and condition $c$, with rate
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{m,t,c} = \frac{k_{m,t,c}}{n_{t,c}}, \qquad
  k_{m,t,c} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{t,c}} y_i ,
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the success flag of episode $i$. I pool within a family and never across families. Door results and pick-and-place results are reported separately, and there is no overall score.

With $n$ between 30 and 180 and many counts at or near zero, the textbook normal-approximation interval falls apart. I use the Wilson score interval instead~\cite{wilson1927probable}, which behaves well for small samples and proportions near zero~\cite{brown2001interval}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}}, \qquad z = 1.96.
  \label{eq:wilson}
\end{equation}
At $k=0$ it still says something useful; its upper bound becomes $z^2/(n+z^2)$.

Two counts are compared with the two-sided Fisher exact test, and for the two models I add a sign test on the seeds where exactly one of them succeeded. None of these tests is confirmatory. Nothing was pre-registered, I chose the comparisons after seeing the data, and I made no correction for multiple testing. A $p$-value here answers a modest question: how surprising would this difference be if only sampling variation were at work? I state differences between conditions as counts, 15/60 against 10/60, rather than as relative drops, because a ratio of two small rates swings wildly with one extra success.

\section{Validity}
\label{sec:validity}

Sample size is the main limit on comparisons between conditions. At 30 episodes per configuration, a rate near 25\% carries an interval roughly 15 percentage points wide on either side, and several of the contrasts the protocol was built to detect disappear inside that width.

What each condition actually measures depends on which scene directory it loads. Door Layout reuses the training scenes, so it says nothing about new geometry. Pairing can only reveal a drop where Seeds or Pose is above zero to begin with. And no episode starts more than about 2~m from its fixture, so store-scale navigation is never tested.

The question of external validity is, for once, very concrete. These are two particular checkpoints, run on one particular, later version of the benchmark through one particular serving stack. The results speak to whether the released artefacts reproduce the published numbers today. They do not show what Octo or $\pi_{0.5}$ could learn from these demonstrations, and they say nothing about physical robots.

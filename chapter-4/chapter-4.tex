\section{Experimental logic}

A benchmark is only as informative as its ability to explain why an embodied system succeeds or breaks down. In robotics, that diagnostic link is notoriously fragile. A policy might fail because its visual encoder misinterprets a branded logo under novel lighting, because its motion planner clips the edge of a shelf, or simply because its low-level controller cannot track an aggressive trajectory. If the evaluation protocol aggregates these disparate failure modes into a single scalar success percentage, it conceals the very physical insights it was built to expose.

Our methodology bridges the three core research questions to concrete, observable physical metrics. Addressing RQ1 demands verifying that procedural store plans are not just topologically diverse, but geometrically navigable and computationally feasible under batch simulation. Answering RQ2 requires establishing a standardized multi-view inference client capable of serving structurally divergent vision--language--action architectures under identical observation buffers. Finally, answering RQ3 requires a disciplined experimental ladder: evaluating released model checkpoints across strictly controlled environmental perturbations, tracking multi-trial success rates, and subjecting execution traces to rigorous protocol auditing.

The fundamental experimental unit is an individual policy rollout instantiated within a procedurally generated retail environment. Each episode is governed by five coordinates: the target task, the specified product, the destination fixture, the procedural environment seed, and the designated evaluation scenario. Throughout execution, the policy operates as a sensorimotor controller: it consumes only language prompts, synchronized RGB images, and proprioceptive state buffers. All privileged simulator state---exact rigid-body poses, mesh bounding boxes, contact normals, and joint velocities---remains encapsulated within ManiSkill3 for deterministic scene resetting and predicate verification.

Three methodological principles anchor this framework:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Common Observation Contract}: Both evaluated policies interface with identical multi-view visual inputs (left shoulder, right shoulder, in-hand wrist) and proprioception buffers.
  \item \textbf{Invariable Terminal Predicates}: Identical spatial thresholds, velocity limits, and collateral disturbance checks govern success across every evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Strict Procedural Alignment}: Both models are evaluated across an identical suite of 42 atomic task configurations, spanning nominal conditions, local chassis noise, novel store environments, and compositional skill--item pairings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scene synthesis procedure}

Every evaluation scene originates from a procedural configuration file defining boundary dimensions, primary fixture types, and random seed parameters. Generation proceeds sequentially:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item Perimeter walls and structural obstacles are placed, establishing boundary contours.
  \item Orthogonal sweep vectors traverse the floor at fixed spatial strides. At each candidate point, the orientation tensor field (Equations~\ref{eq:basis-tensor}--\ref{eq:tensor-orientation}) is queried lazily against boundary segments.
  \item Candidates aligning within roughly $37^{\circ}$ of a Cartesian axis are snapped to that axis; non-conforming or colliding candidates are rejected.
  \item Accepted shelving units are concatenated longitudinally to form structured multi-unit runs, bounded by a mandatory 2.0-meter aisle passage envelope.
  \item Support surfaces on accepted fixtures are detected, and retail products are populated onto regular Cartesian grids subject to bounded spatial noise ($\pm 1.5$~cm, $\pm 5^{\circ}$).
  \item Wall, floor, ceiling, and fixture materials are sampled from independent texture pools.
\end{enumerate}

This pipeline distinguishes two failure types: candidate rejection and scene rejection. A candidate rejection is normal; the generator discards the invalid footprint and continues sweeping. A scene rejection occurs when the generator fails to satisfy aisle clearance or fixture density targets within twenty restarts. In that event, the seed is abandoned. This distinction ensures that geometric impossibilities are filtered out before evaluation, preventing flawed scene synthesis from masquerading as a policy manipulation failure.

Scene validity is corroborated through two distinct checks. First, hard geometric collision routines and minimum-clearance bounds ensure physical accessibility by construction. Second, simulation throughput tests benchmark GPU stepping performance across increasing fixture densities, ensuring that complex multi-shelf scenes remain computationally stable.

\section{Demonstration structure and data provenance}

In the upstream RoboBenchMart design, demonstration collection relies on staged kinematic planning. Every atomic task is decomposed into a structured sequence of discrete operational phases: base navigation, pre-grasp alignment, grasp execution, object lift, transport, pre-place alignment, and gripper release.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/figures/retail-vla-benchmark/collector_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{The automated demonstration collection pipeline. Privileged simulator state seeds intermediate waypoint anchors around the target product; fixed-base segments are solved via analytical screw interpolation, with an RRT-Connect planner catching kinematically complex trajectories. Segments requiring base displacement invoke an approach planner. Only rollouts that complete all stages and pass strict terminal predicates are committed to the training corpus; all failed attempts are discarded.}
    \label{fig:anchor-sequence}
\end{figure}

\ref{fig:anchor-sequence} illustrates the collection architecture. For each phase, task-specific logic computes target waypoint anchors using privileged object poses. When the mobile base remains stationary, the sampler computes an analytical screw motion trajectory. The proposed path is checked for collisions against scene geometry and neighboring stock. If a collision is detected, the planner attempts a second screw motion; if that fails, execution falls back to RRT-Connect, which conducts a bidirectional randomized search through the robot's 7-DOF arm configuration space. When base repositioning is required, a dedicated non-holonomic approach planner guides the differential-drive base into an optimal reach envelope.

The proposed trajectory is executed in ManiSkill3 with full rigid-body dynamics enabled. If any segment collides, if kinematic limits are breached, or if the terminal success predicate is not satisfied, the entire episode is discarded, the simulator resets, and the sampling loop restarts.

This all-or-nothing filtering guarantees that saved demonstrations represent clean, collision-free executions. Crucially, it also introduces an inherent selection bias: the training corpus consists exclusively of nominal, planner-optimal paths. It contains zero failed grasps, zero recovery trajectories, and zero corrective realignment maneuvers. Consequently, imitation models trained on this corpus receive no supervision indicating how to return to a nominal trajectory when slight controller inaccuracies induce drift. In our local reproduction environment, the 2,976 demonstration metadata headers document this upstream training distribution, providing the foundational context for the released checkpoints evaluated herein.

\section{Model checkpoints and inference protocol}
\label{sec:training}

Rather than conducting local training runs from scratch, this dissertation focuses on evaluating publicly released, fine-tuned checkpoints provided by the upstream RoboBenchMart project. This experimental design isolates policy execution from the confounding stochasticity of unverified local training runs.

We evaluate two prominent, structurally divergent vision--language--action architectures:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo} (93M parameters): A transformer-based diffusion policy combining visual tokenizers, language conditioning, and a continuous diffusion action head. We evaluate the released fine-tuned checkpoint (\texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_octo}, git revision \texttt{f3c8de450fc08fb5737d32442a2b1e6ab2993244}, checkpoint \texttt{1000000}). The policy is served via \texttt{scripts/octo\_server.py}. In our runtime audit, we confirmed that the evaluation server runs with an observation history window of one frame ($H_{\text{obs}} = 1$), commanding continuous action chunks of 50 steps.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$}: A state-of-the-art vision--language--action model integrating a PaliGemma vision--language backbone with a specialized continuous flow-matching action expert. We evaluate the released fine-tuned weights (\texttt{emb-ai/RoboBenchMart\_pi05}, git revision \texttt{533ad107347c3568dc2456d1586b665889f0ed18}), deployed via the OpenPI server architecture under configuration \texttt{pi05\_eval\_rbm}. The model processes current camera inputs and language prompts to generate 50-step action chunks.
\end{enumerate}

Both models execute in ManiSkill3 via client--server remote procedure calls. A crucial runtime distinction emerged during execution:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Octo Execution}: Completed under batch identifier \texttt{octo\_parallel\_20260928\_021558}. The first 17 configurations were executed serially; the remaining 25 configurations were executed across four independent, parallel Octo server instances.
  \item \textbf{$\boldsymbol{\pi_{0.5}}$ Execution}: Completed under batch identifier \texttt{pi05\_parallel\_20261002\_105333}. Four independent simulation client processes ran concurrently, all interfacing with a single shared OpenPI server process on port 8000.
\end{itemize}

Because pseudorandom number consumption and asynchronous server queuing differ between these two execution frameworks, this evaluation should not be interpreted as a strictly paired, episode-by-episode comparison. Instead, it constitutes a standardized benchmark comparing the operational capabilities of both model pipelines across identical task configurations.

Episode execution is governed by explicit simulation horizon budgets: Board and Floor tasks enforce a maximum of 750 simulator steps; Basket picking enforces 600 steps; Refrigerator tasks enforce 500 steps; and Showcase tasks enforce 1,000 steps. In all cases, policies execute an action chunk of 50 steps open-loop before a new visual observation is returned.

\section{Evaluation scenarios and configuration matrix}
\label{sec:scenarios}

We evaluate both models across a comprehensive matrix of 42 atomic task configurations defined in \texttt{bash/eval\_model.sh}. The configurations are organized into four progressive distribution-shift tiers:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Roman*.},leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Seeds} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_train} suffix): Re-instantiates the nominal store plans, fixture placements, and product arrangements used during demonstration collection. This tier tests in-domain imitation fidelity under nominal initial states.
  \item \textbf{Pose} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_robo} suffix): Preserves the training store layouts, textures, and product identities, but perturbs the robot's initial chassis placement in front of the fixture ($\pm 25$~cm translation, $\pm 22.5^{\circ}$ yaw; seed 10000). This tests whether the policy can adjust to local base positioning offsets.
  \item \textbf{Layout} (12 configurations; \texttt{\_uns} suffix; seed 42000): Introduces novel floor plans alongside unseen wall, floor, and ceiling texture sets. The target tasks and products remain familiar, but surrounding visual clutter and lighting perspectives change.
  \item \textbf{Pairing} (6 configurations; \texttt{\_ood} suffix; seed 42000): Evaluates zero-shot task--item composition. The model is commanded to manipulate a target product that was present in training demonstrations for \emph{other} tasks, but was never demonstrated for the evaluated task (e.g., placing a Nivea cream box on a shelf board, or picking a Slam juice into a cart).
\end{enumerate}

Two important methodological caveats govern this matrix. First, door manipulation tasks (Open and Close) have no meaningful Pairing condition because their instructions address fixed fixture handles rather than product entities; these cells are marked not applicable (--). Second, our protocol audit revealed that in door tasks, the nominal Layout condition does not instantiate disjoint store plans; it reuses the training floor layouts, varying only textures and starting poses. Consequently, door Layout tests texture invariance rather than geometric transfer.

Each configuration is evaluated over 30 independent Monte Carlo rollouts. Performance is aggregated across canonical task families:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Basket} (Pick to Basket; 3 items: Fanta, Nivea, Honey Stars): Pools 3 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (90 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Nestl{\'e}, Slam).
  \item \textbf{Board} (Board to Board; 3 items: Duff, Nestl{\'e}, Vanish): Pools 3 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (90 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Nivea, Fanta).
  \item \textbf{Floor} (Pick from Floor; 2 items: Heinz Beans, Slam): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each), and 2 configurations under Pairing (60 episodes; Fanta, Duff).
  \item \textbf{Open} (Fridge and Showcase Open): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each).
  \item \textbf{Close} (Fridge and Showcase Close): Pools 2 configurations under Seeds, Pose, and Layout (60 episodes each).
\end{itemize}

This yields 360 episodes under Seeds, 360 under Pose, 360 under Layout, and 180 under Pairing, totaling exactly 1,260 evaluated episodes per model (2,520 episodes in aggregate).

\section{Success and side-effect checks}

Evaluating retail manipulation requires monitoring whole-scene integrity. Knocking over adjacent stock to reach a target item constitutes an operational failure.

The evaluation engine enforces the strict composite predicate formulated in Equation~\ref{eq:predicate-success}:
\begin{equation}
  y = \mathbb{I}[o^* \in G]\;\mathbb{I}[\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\text{robot}}\rVert_2 \leq v_{\text{tol}}]\;\prod_{o \in \mathcal{N}(o^*)} \mathbb{I}[\lVert\Delta \mathbf{p}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_p \;\land\; \lVert\Delta \mathbf{q}_o\rVert_{\infty} \le \epsilon_q].
  \label{eq:strict-success}
\end{equation}
The target item $o^*$ must settle within destination volume $G$, the robot platform must settle below velocity threshold $v_{\text{tol}} = 0.2$~m/s, and collateral objects $o \in \mathcal{N}(o^*)$ must not exceed translational ($\epsilon_p = 0.1$~m) or rotational ($\epsilon_q = 0.1$) displacement tolerances.

Our audit surfaced the exact geometric tolerances implemented in code:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Basket Destination}: Evaluated as an axis-aligned bounding cube of $0.4\times 0.4\times 0.4$~meters anchored to the robot's base coordinates (expanded to $0.5$~m for Honey Stars). Collateral checking monitors only items residing on the target fixture shelf.
  \item \textbf{Board Transfer Destination}: Enforces a horizontal window of $\pm 0.1$~m centered $0.397$~meters vertically above the source shelf, with scene-wide collateral monitoring.
  \item \textbf{Door Articulation}: Showcase doors must reach $1.57 \pm 0.2$~rad (open) or $0 \pm 0.2$~rad (close); refrigerator covers require $0.624 \pm 0.1$~rad.
\end{itemize}

Success rates are reported as exact counts alongside percentages. Absolute percentage-point deltas quantify degradation between conditions:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S_{m,t} = S_{m,t,\mathrm{Pose}} - S_{m,t,\mathrm{Layout}}.
  \label{eq:degradation}
\end{equation}

\section{Outcome logging and artifact traceability}

To ensure complete experimental auditability without relying on opaque summaries, all 2,520 rollouts were logged with verified artifact manifests.

Each of the 42 configurations generates an execution log containing an explicit terminal summary (\texttt{Results: [list of successful episodes]}). Each rollout produces a full MP4 video recording, indexed by configuration and episode number. In the project repository, \texttt{episode\_outcomes.csv} maps every rollout to its terminal binary outcome, while \texttt{artifact\_manifest.csv} and \texttt{log\_manifest.csv} document the SHA-256 checksums, byte sizes, and paths of all 2,688 generated output files (1,344 files per model, including 1,260 MP4 videos stored via Git LFS).

Our inspection identified a crucial data boundary: while video recordings and text logs are complete, container HDF5 files are exactly 800 bytes each, and JSON \texttt{episodes} arrays are unpopulated. Consequently, the archive does not preserve continuous per-step joint trajectories. Episode success indices are derived directly from verified runtime environment logs.

\section{Validity considerations}
\label{sec:validity}

Evaluating robotic policies in simulation involves critical validity boundaries:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Construct Validity}: While the generator synthesizes large-scale $24\times 15$-meter supermarkets, policy evaluation does not exercise whole-store navigation. Every episode begins with the robot positioned 1.4~meters in front of the active fixture, facing it. Aisle geometry and surrounding shelving provide visual background and local approach constraints, but policies are never required to traverse store-scale routes. Non-rigid packaging and human obstacles are excluded.
  \item \textbf{Internal Validity}: The evaluated checkpoints represent public fine-tuned weights rather than locally trained models. Furthermore, two operational constraints dominate the control loop: (1) policies execute open-loop action chunks of 50 simulation steps between visual observations, blinding them to transient contact events, and (2) client logic explicitly overwrites the torso-lift and head-pan action elements with zero at every timestep ($q_{\text{torso}} = 0$), permanently locking the robot into an immobilized vertical spine.
  \item \textbf{External Validity}: Rigid-body simulation in ManiSkill3 does not capture sensor noise, tactile compliance, packaging deformation, or motor backlash inherent to physical hardware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol traceability and quality control}
\label{sec:traceability}

A foundational objective of this dissertation is establishing protocol hygiene. By verifying execution logs against SHA-256 manifests, linking binary outcomes to archived Git LFS videos, and documenting the precise client-side control clamps, this framework ensures that all reported findings remain fully auditable and reproducible across the research community.
